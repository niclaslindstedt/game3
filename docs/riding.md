# Riding

The craft model (`engine/game/craft.ts`, with `hull.ts`, `hydro.ts`, `propulsion.ts`, `flight.ts`, `assist.ts`, `collision.ts`; numbers in `engine/game/defs/tuning.ts` and the catalog in `defs/craft.ts`) is a rigid body afloat, integrated semi-implicitly at 120 Hz. Every force is summed in `stepCraft`, each from the module that owns its model, and each model names its source at the function that implements it. **There is no MODE.** A launch is a hull whose probes all came out of the water; a landing is one whose probes went back in; a dive is a landing whose bow went in first. Each is read off the same forces every other step is made of, and the events say so after the fact. The one exception is the rider righting a capsized hull (below), which is a half-second in which nothing is a force.

Every number below is quoted with its unit as `TUNING` states it; the per-craft ones are the catalog's, and the measured ones at the end come from riding the real engine on the synthetic drag strip the tests use.

## The rider first

The rider is a point mass in the spec (`riderMass` kg, `riderHeight` m above the centre of gravity) that nothing draws yet and the physics still carries. The inputs move it, slower than a thumb: full lean back or forward shifts it `rider.leanReach` = 0.55 m aft or forward, full steer hangs it `rider.leanIn` = 0.28 m into the turn, and both lag with a time constant of `rider.leanLag` = 0.18 s. **Both stand down as the hull changes ends**: they are scaled by how much of the craft's way is still forwards, from all of it to none by `rider.asternFade` = 1 m/s astern, because sliding back down the seat is how a rider unsticks a bow he is driving under and hanging off is how he holds a carve — a craft crawling backwards off a mark at walking pace is doing neither, and a rider still hung off one held the skiff at 53° of steady heel while it did. The way is read off the craft's own nose taken out of `q` and flattened — never off `heading`, which `toEuler` swings 180° as the pitch folds at ±90° — and it applies AFLOAT ONLY, because a hull inverted at the top of a flip really is travelling backwards along its nose and has no water under it to go astern through. A rider who is TUCKED has less of both to give — see the tuck, below — and one who has STOOD UP has far more of the first, which is what puts the craft on its tail. Gravity on the two masses (the rider's at `riderHeight`, the hull's balanced below it so the pair cancels at neutral) turns the shift into a trim moment — which is the whole of what `lean` does afloat, and `tests/craft_test.ts` holds that leaning back at speed lifts the nose.

## The hull as probes (`hull.ts`)

The hull is 22 probes laid out from the spec's `length`, `beam`, `height` and `deadrise`: six stations along the keel at `hull.stations` = 0.06, 0.22, 0.4, 0.58, 0.76, 0.92 of the length from the transom, each a keel probe and two chine probes (`chineOut` = 0.8 of the half-beam, drawn in by `stationTaper` toward the bow, lifted by the deadrise), plus four deck probes at the sheer — the gunwales, both sides, fore and aft — owning the sealed volume above the bottom. Each probe owns a share of the hull's volume (`stationShare`, aft-heavy — a planing hull carries its volume aft; `keelShare` = 0.4 of a station on the keel), a share of its bottom area (the plan area is `length × beam × 0.72`), and a share of its lateral projection (`lateralStationShare` = 0.35, 0.28, 0.17, 0.1, 0.06, 0.04 — the sponsons are at the stern and the bow is out at speed, so the lateral centre lands behind the centre of gravity and a yawed hull weathervanes rather than spins). The bow's rise is `stationRise` = 0, 0, 0, 0, 0.12, 0.42 of the hull depth, which gives the forward probes a slope. The deck probes hold `deckShare` = 0.55 of the displacement so an inverted hull still floats (a PWC does not self-right from all the way over; the rider flips it after a moment — the capsize, below); upright they never fill.

Every step, `placeProbes` puts each probe in the world off the body's pose and rates (`v_p = v + R(ω × p)`) and the craft reads `surfaceAt` under every one — the height, the normal AND the Lagrangian surface velocity (wave orbit, Stokes drift and current), so every water force below is computed against water that is itself moving and being transported. The inertia tensor is the hull as a box of its own dimensions plus the rider's mass at `riderHeight` on the pitch and roll axes (`inertia`); `totalMass` is hull plus rider.

**Buoyancy (Archimedes).** `F = ρ·g·V·fill` straight up per probe, `fill` the linear clip of the probe's depth under the surface over its band (0.72 of the hull depth for a bottom probe, 0.3 for a deck probe); the torque falls out of where the probe sits. At rest the craft floats where Archimedes puts it — `restY(spec, density)` bisects the level, still-water immersion at which the probes' volume × density = mass — and `tests/buoyancy_test.ts` holds a settled craft to it, holds a denser hull lower, rights a heeled one and floats one all the way over. `standCraft` stands every craft at `restY` above the surface; a craft placed under way sits `hull.planingRise` = 0.3 m higher so it is not dropped into the water at seventy an hour.

**The drags**, per wet probe, on the relative flow resolved into the body frame:

- _Skin friction_ along the hull: `−½·ρ·C_F·A·|u|·u` over the wet patch (a patch is wet in AREA as soon as the surface reaches `patchWet` = 0.3 of the hull depth — the friction's measure, where the volume fill is the buoyancy's), with `C_F` the ITTC-57 model-ship correlation line, `C_F = 0.075 / (log₁₀ Re − 2)²`, `Re = u·L/ν`, `ν` = `water.viscosity` = 1.14·10⁻⁶ m²/s, floored at Re = 10⁵.
- _Residuary (form) drag_ in displacement mode: a bluff-body drag `formCd` = 0.14 on the submerged frontal section (`beam × immersion × lateralShare`), sized so the hump costs ~15 % of the weight at C_v ≈ 1 as Savitsky's hump data has it. It fades with `(1 − hump)`, where `hump` is whichever comes first — the eased `planing` readout (the lift carrying the weight) or the speed's place in Savitsky's band, `(C_v − fadeLow) / (fadeHigh − fadeLow)` clipped to 0..1 — past the hump the bottom is a lifting surface and the lift's tilt is the pressure drag.
- _Lateral_: the keel and the sponsons as a plate against the sideways flow, `−½·ρ·lateralCd·A_lat·|u_r|·u_r` with `A_lat = length × immersion × lateralShare` and `lateralCd` the spec's — the force that makes the hull carve rather than skate.
- _Heave_: the bottom as a flat plate moving normal to itself, `heaveCd` = 1.15 (Hoerner 1965 ~1.17).
- _The bow's lift_: the rising bottom forward meets the flow at its slope plus the local trim, and the pressure on an inclined plate — Newtonian impact theory's `C_p = 2·sin²σ` (Hayes & Probstein 1959), scaled by `bowCp` = 1 — acts normal to the bottom: mostly up, a little back. This is the bow wave's lift, what trims a hull nose-up through the hump before Savitsky's lift takes the weight, and what a bow buried at speed is pushed back out by, and decelerated by.
- _The dive's drag_: a section immersed past `diveDepth` = 0.75 of the hull depth is BURIED and pushes water ahead of itself as the bluff body it then is, `diveCd` = 0.7 on the buried frontal area — the deceleration of a dive. The buried extent is capped at the probe's own band, because that is the whole section the hull has to push with: a hull a metre under presents the same area as one just buried, only deeper, and charged by depth without a bound it brakes a stuffed bow at eleven g.
- _The chine's bank-in_: the sideways flow meets the V panel on the side the hull slides toward, and that panel's normal has an upward part of `tan(deadrise)` per unit of lateral force (`chineBank` = 1 is the geometry). Lifting the outer chine rolls the hull INTO the turn, which is what a PWC does and a keel-level lateral force alone would do the opposite of.
- _Rotational damping_ beyond the probes': `hull.rotDamp` = 300 / 150 / 400 N·m·s about pitch / yaw / roll, scaled by how wet the hull is — the added-mass damping a dozen point drags under-count. Yaw is the smallest of the three because the water a rotating hull has to shift is the area it sweeps normal to the motion: pitching and rolling sweep the bottom (length × beam), while yawing sweeps only the lateral profile (length × immersion), a strip a couple of tenths of a metre deep once the hull is on the plane.

**Slamming (von Kármán 1929).** A probe ENTERING the water — within 2 cm of the surface or under it, its band not yet filled, and closing on the water — feels the wedge impact: the added mass of the entering wedge grows with the wetted half-width `c = V·t·cot β`, an average pressure of `½·ρ·V²·(π·cot β)` over the strip, applied over the part of the probe's patch still to be wetted `(1 − fill)` and scaled by `slamShare` = 0.35 (a hull landing at speed meets the water progressively, not flat). The closing speed `V` is the LESSER of two, both against the flow relative to the water: how fast the probe is being driven INTO the water, and the closing normal to the bottom (along the hull's up). The first is read along the water SURFACE's own normal rather than the world's vertical, because that projection is exactly the rate the probe's immersion grows — differentiate `depth = η(x, z, t) − y` along the probe's path, substitute the free surface's kinematic condition `w = η_t + u·η_x + v·η_z`, and `−rel·n` over the unnormalised normal `(−η_x, 1, −η_z)` is what is left, with the vertical reading its `η_x = η_z = 0` special case. On level water it is the same number either way; on a wave FACE it is not, and read vertically a hull driving into the back of the wave ahead in a following sea — the water under it falling away while the face rises into its path — registers as receding from water it is closing on at several metres a second, so no slam fires and the bow knifes in. A bow driven in nose-down has its bottom moving away from the water and takes no slam there — the deck does the scooping, and buries; a hull on the plane has its bottom closing on the flow at its trim every step, which is the lift Savitsky already prices and no slam at all; a hull arriving flat has both and meets the water square. The force acts along the hull's up, so a wedge entering nose-down pushes the bow back up — the pitch-up a flat landing recovers by, and the pitch-down a buried bow does not get. The whole hull's slam is capped at `slamCapG` = 7 g of the total weight (the pile-up Wagner 1932 doubles `c` by is a pressure real hulls spread and riders' knees absorb), and the fastest closing among the entering probes (`entryVy`) is what the `land` event reports as `vy` when it is steeper than the body's own descent.

**Planing lift (Savitsky 1964, `hydro.ts`).** The lift coefficient of a prismatic planing surface at trim `τ` (degrees) and mean wetted length-to-beam ratio `λ` at speed coefficient `C_v = V/√(g·B)`:

```
C_L0 = τ^1.1 · (0.0120·λ^0.5 + 0.0055·λ^2.5 / C_v²)
C_Lβ = C_L0 − 0.0065·β·C_L0^0.6        (β the deadrise, degrees)
L    = ½·ρ·V²·B²·C_Lβ                   along the hull's up
```

The lift is faded in over `planing.fadeLow..fadeHigh` = C_v 0.5–1.4 (Savitsky's data starts at ~0.6; below it the hull is in displacement mode and the probes are the whole story), `τ` clamped to `trimMin..trimMax` = 1.5–14° with the lift scaled linearly to zero under the floor, `λ` clamped to `lambdaMin..lambdaMax` = 0.4–4 with the same scaling under the floor, and `C_Lβ` capped at `clMax` = 0.35 (the formula runs away at high trim and low speed, where a real hull would be porpoising). The wetted length is read off the keel's immersion at the transom and the bow stations (`wettedLength`), continuous in the hull's pose so the trim settles rather than hunts. The lift's tilt by the trim IS the method's induced drag `W·tan τ`.

It is applied in `hullForces` as a **strip model**: each wet bottom probe moving forward through the water lifts by the coefficient its OWN local flow angle earns (`trim = atan2(−u_up, u_fwd)` at the probe, against the whole hull's wetted length), weighted by its share of the bottom — its area times `liftAft + (1 − liftAft) · station`, rising toward the bow; `liftAft` = 0.15 is the taper's floor at the transom, where a clean Kutta transom would be 0 — times how wet its patch is (`patchWet`, the friction's measure). Every bottom probe is counted in the denominator of the shares, wet or not: a strip lifts only while it is in the water, so a hull with half its bottom clear carries half the lift, not all of it on whatever is left wet. A probe moving down into the water meets it at a steeper angle and lifts harder. **Past a trim there is no planing surface left to earn lift at all**: the coefficient's band is clamped at both ends, so a hull reared onto its tail was being evaluated at `τ = trimMax` and carrying its full planing weight on a bottom pointing at the sky. The lift is now scaled out from `planing.fadeFrom` = 30° of the probe's own flow angle to nothing by `planing.fadeGone` = 55°, where the flow has separated off the chine and the stern's buoyancy carries the craft instead. The fade starts well above `trimMax` = 14° on purpose — Savitsky's data ending at 15° means the polynomial is unvouched for above it, not that the lift stops, and a ramp or a landing runs past it and does still plane; fading from `trimMax` itself cost the marlin a third of its launches (`make sim`). That is the planing surface's heave and pitch damping falling out of the geometry rather than being added, and what keeps the hull from porpoising. The resultant stands at the wetted bottom's centroid rather than at Savitsky's `0.75·λ·B`; `pressureCentre` in `hydro.ts` computes the latter and is exported, but nothing in the physics reads it. An inverted hull's bottom is in the air (`up.y` ≤ 0.2): no planing lift. `hullForces` also reports `liftX`, the wetness-weighted lateral centre of those shares (body right positive); it is computed for the ride lab and read by nothing in the physics. The `planing` readout (0..1, the lift's share of the weight) follows the lift at `planingFollow` = 6 /s for the HUD and the spray.

## The pump (`propulsion.ts`)

A personal watercraft has no propeller, no rudder and no gears: an axial-flow pump draws water through a flush intake under the transom and throws it out of a nozzle, and the nozzle swings to steer. Three consequences the whole handling model rests on: thrust falls as the hull speeds up, thrust vanishes the moment the intake leaves the water, and with no thrust there is almost nothing to steer with.

- **Momentum theory** (Allison 1993; Bulten 2006 ch. 2): the jet velocity `V_j = impellerPitch · rpm / 60`, the flow `Q = A_nozzle · V_j` (`A_nozzle = π·d²/4` from the spec's `nozzleDiameter`), and the thrust `T = η_t · ρ · Q · (V_j − V_in)`, with the inflow `V_in = inflowFactor · V_through_water`, `inflowFactor` = 0.9 (the boundary layer under the hull slows the intake's inflow), and the overall thrust efficiency `thrustEfficiency` = 0.66 for the duct, nozzle and impeller losses (marine waterjets run 0.6–0.75; a PWC's short flush intake is the poorer). Zero with the intake out of the water.

- **Neutral**: zero forward or reverse request means zero NET axial propulsion while the engine and pump remain at idle. Current operator guidance for gated personal watercraft specifies adjusting neutral until the craft does not move at idle, while warning that wind and current can still carry it; this model represents that force balance directly rather than resolving the gate's narrow neutral angle. A forward or reverse request exposes the pump's gross thrust again. The engine note and pump load therefore remain alive at neutral without a hidden 2.16 m/s crawl overwhelming the environmental drift.

- **The intake's RAM DRAG** (`intakeDrag`): that same difference `V_j − V_in` CHANGES SIGN. With the throttle shut and the engine back at idle the jet is slower than the water coming in, and the duct is taking momentum out of the hull rather than adding it. `thrust` returns the positive half of the expression and `intakeDrag` the negative one, as a magnitude, because they do not act in the same place: the ram drag is the INTAKE's, ahead of the pump, so the nozzle does not aim it and the bucket cannot turn it forward — it pulls along the hull's own line, below the centre of gravity at the transom, whatever the bars and the gate are doing. Exactly one of the two is ever non-zero, so nothing is counted twice. It is what a rider feels the INSTANT a thumb comes off the throttle, before the hull has begun to come off the plane: on the skiff at 95 km/h it is about a tenth of a g on its own. The intake is fed while the transom-station keel is within `pump.intakeDepth` = 0.05 m of the surface, and the engine has a tilt cut-off: a capsized craft's throttle is closed.
- **The pump load**: the jet's kinetic power `½·ρ·Q·V_j²` over `pumpEfficiency` = 0.86, which is ∝ rpm³, so the shaft torque is ∝ rpm². With the intake dry it falls to `airLoad` = 0.08 of that and the engine runs up to the limiter — `tests/craft_test.ts` holds an airborne engine at > 98 % of redline.
- **The engine**: the spec's torque curve (a marine four-stroke's shape — rising to a plateau at 70 % of redline and falling to the rated power at `maxRpm`) times the lagged throttle (`throttleLag` = 0.12 s), less a friction torque of `friction` = 0.12 of peak torque at redline growing linearly with rpm; the shaft (`inertia` = 0.06 kg·m²) integrates `(T_engine − T_pump)/I`. The pump term is integrated IMPLICITLY — its stiffness at redline is several times the step's reciprocal, and an explicit step would ring. The idle governor holds `idleRpm` and the limiter `maxRpm`.
- **Forced induction** (`boost`, per craft): a centrifugal blower is geared off the crank, so its delivery pressure rises with the square of shaft speed. The engine's torque is multiplied by `1 + peak·((rpm/maxRpm − onset)/(1 − onset))²` above the onset and by nothing below it. The catalog's curve is built for the power the engine makes WITHOUT the blower, so the rated figure still lands at redline and the midrange is the poorer for it — a blown craft of a given rated power is softer down low than an unblown one of the same rated power, which is the whole trade. Only the marlin is blown (175 kW at the limiter out of a 121 kW engine, nothing under 55 % of the rev range); `ratedTorque` is the boosted torque at redline, and the pump-match test is quoted against it.
- **The reverse bucket** (`bucket`, per craft): a clamshell that drops over the nozzle and throws the jet forward and down. It is the ONLY brake a watercraft has and the only reverse. `c.bucket` (0..1) follows the brake lever, and HOW it follows is the throw described below; with it `d` down, the axial thrust is `(1 − d) − d·reverse` of the jet's — through zero at a neutral part way down and to `−reverse` of full thrust at the stop. The nozzle is UPSTREAM of the gate, but what the gate reverses is the flow's axial sense and not the side of the transom it leaves from: it catches a jet already thrown to one side and its walls send it forward on that same side. Reversing a vector's axial component does not touch its lateral one, so the STEERING reaction keeps the same sign AND the same magnitude however far down the gate is — `bucketVector` returns no sideways share at all, and the side force is the whole jet's. A brake pulled with way still on therefore turns the way the bars are pointing, and turns better than either a coast or the throttle (`brakeSteer`, below). The inversion a rider feels in reverse is not a flipped moment: it is a hull travelling stern-first, where a bow swung right walks the craft to the left, and it needs no model of its own. The deflected share leaves downward under the transom (`pump.bucketDown` = 0.55 of it), and its reaction lifts the stern, so braking puts the BOW DOWN; the reverse thrust acts below the centre of gravity and pushes the same way. Pulling the lever also opens the throttle to `pump.bucketThrottle` = 0.65 on its own, because the gate can only turn flow the pump is already making. `reverse: 0` is a craft with no bucket fitted — the stand-up — which therefore has neither a brake nor a reverse.

- **THE JAB — the throttling half of the gate's travel is thrown and the reversing half is servoed** (`stepBucket`, `bucketNeutral`, `pump.jabGate` = 0.7 / `pump.jabShut` = 0.15). Everything from shut down to the gate's own NEUTRAL — `1 / (1 + reverse)`, 0.63–0.69 across the roster — spoils thrust, hangs the plate in the stream and squats the stern, and reverses nothing; everything past it is sternway. Swinging through that first half at `deploy` (0.3–0.45 s) meant a short jab spent itself arriving: the skiff reached its own neutral **0.300 s** after the ask, so a third of a second on the lever was worth nothing at all, and the brake read as mush exactly where it is most used — placing the craft between two buoys, or swinging the bow back at one just missed. So a lever asked for IN FULL drives that half at `pump.jabRate` = 2 times the servo's rate — 0.150 s across it on the slowest gate against 0.300 — and only neutral to the stop, the part that has to turn the flow rather than merely split it, keeps `deploy`. **Coming back UP, `pump.jabShut` drives the WHOLE travel**, and the asymmetry is the stream's: a clamshell full of jet is BLOWN open where it had to be levered shut. It is also the direction a rider can least afford to wait on — he is off the brake because he wants the throttle back — and it is worth the most: the gate is clear 0.225 s after the key comes up rather than 0.450 on the skiff, and the speed shed between letting go and going forward again falls from 8.7 km/h to 4.7 (marlin 10.0 → 8.0, otter 5.8 → 3.2). **It is a RATE and not a jump**, which is load-bearing rather than fastidious: every force the gate carries is read by the rider, whose body is on springs and may not move in steps (`tests/rider_test.ts` holds the torso to a degree per 120 Hz tick while the craft gathers way astern). That reading is linear in `jabRate` — 0.52° at 1, 0.75° at 2, 0.98° at 3, 1.21° at 4 — so 2 is the honest ceiling and the headroom above it belongs to the SNAP that test exists to catch, not to this. A quicker gate than this means sizing the rider's springs in the same change. A lever held BETWEEN the two gates never throws and swings the whole travel at the servo's rate, both ways: feathering the gate is still feathering it, and the throw is what a committed hand is paid — the same bargain `flight.pumpGate` strikes at the top of the lean axis. The keyboard's own ramp is the other half of that pair (`KEY_REVERSE_ATTACK` = `KEY_REVERSE_RELEASE` = 30 in `input-model.ts`, the quickest axis in the game and the only one whose halves are equal): the axis is sampled once a physics step, so the gate is crossed five steps in — 0.04 s. Its RELEASE has no ramp under it at all: the key drops the lever to 0 on the step it comes up, because on this axis alone the release is also a REQUEST — `reverse > 0` is what holds the throttle shut, so a decaying tail is a rider asking for the gas and being handed neither the gas nor the brake. A thumb on the glass stays analogue. A gate the ramp cannot reach inside a jab is a throw no keyboard rider would ever earn, so `tests/input_model_test.ts` reads both numbers and holds them together.

- **The gate as a PLATE IN THE WATER** (`bucketDrag`): the other half of the brake, and at speed the larger one. A deployed bucket is not only a mirror held up to the jet — it is a bluff body hanging in the stream behind the transom, meeting the HULL's speed rather than the pump's flow. It has to be modelled separately because jet thrust FALLS as the hull speeds up (`V_in` closes on `V_j`), so a brake made only of reversed thrust is weakest exactly where a rider reaches for it: before this term the gate was worth about a twentieth of a g at the top of the range, and dropping it at ninety was something a rider could not feel. The plate's drag area (`C_d·A`) is quoted as `pump.bucketDragArea` = 1.1 multiples of the nozzle's own area, scaled by how far down the gate has swung and by that craft's `bucket.reverse` — the same measure of how much of the flow it is standing in. Going as v², it is about a fifth of a g on the skiff at 95 km/h and nothing at all at the walking pace reverse runs at, which is what lets one gate be both a brake and a reverse. It acts aft along the hull's line below the centre of gravity, so it also puts the bow down — braking hard on a watercraft buries the nose, and this is most of why.
- **Variable trim** (`trimRange`, per craft, 0–7°): the nozzle pivots vertically, so the thrust line can leave the hull's axis. The rider's lean carries it (`c.trim` lags at `pump.trimRate` = 1.4 ranges/s); aimed UP the reaction is downward at the transom, and a downward force behind the centre of gravity LIFTS the bow — so leaning back both shifts the rider aft and trims up, and the two agree. The stand-up has no trim system; its rider is the trim.
- The jet leaves the transom turned by the nozzle and the reaction on the hull is its opposite, applied 0.1 m ahead of the transom and 0.1 m above the keel, so a nozzle swung for a clockwise turn throws the jet to the right-rear and pushes the stern LEFT.

`staticThrust` is the pull at the dock at redline; `jetCeiling` (in `limits.ts`) is the jet's own speed at redline, which nothing pushes the hull past. The catalog test holds every pump matched to its engine at the limiter (pump torque 0.85–1.05 × engine torque) and its static pull to 0.6–1.6 × the weight — a jet ski, not a tug.

## The speed class (`craftAtClass`, `CLASS_BAND`)

**One knob makes every craft on the roster faster or slower together** — a race class, the way the sport's own ladder runs — and it is the knob the open ocean's biggest wave is sized off ([water.md](water.md): the ceiling is quadratic in the top speed) AND the knob the course is drawn to (R32 in [level-generator.md](level-generator.md)).

The rider picks it on the craft card, as **CLASS**: `CLASS_BAND` is the four rungs a build offers — NOVICE 0.75, STOCK 1, LIMITED 1.25, OPEN 1.5 — and `settings.ride.speedClass` remembers it. `TUNING.pump.speedClass` is only the DEFAULT a caller gets when it passes none.

It is quoted in what it BUYS: 1.4 means every hull runs 1.4× the speed the catalog quotes it at. Underneath it is a taller impeller and the engine to swing it — the pitch goes as `speedClass^(1/classGain)` and the engine's torque as the cube of that pitch, because the pump's load torque goes as pitch³ at a given shaft speed and an engine that did not grow with it would simply bog. (Measured without that cube, a class of 2 left the roster's fastest craft SLOWER than class 1.) `classGain` = 1.2 is why the pitch is the smaller number: a planing hull lifts as it speeds up, so its wetted area shrinks, its drag grows slower than v², and a pitch that doubles the jet buys more than double the speed. Measured top speed, km/h, flat out on the calm strip:

| class  | 0.75 | 1.00 | 1.15 | 1.30 | 1.50 | 1.75 | 2.00 |
| ------ | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
| skiff  | 58   | 95   | 112  | 127  | 146  | 171  | 196  |
| marlin | 62   | 108  | 126  | 143  | 164  | 197  | 209  |
| otter  | 57   | 91   | 107  | 121  | 141  | 163  | 189  |
| dart   | 50   | 78   | 92   | 105  | 122  | 143  | 168  |

At 1 every craft reproduces its catalog `topSpeed` exactly, and what the class promises tracks what it delivers to within 2–4 % over 1–2. Past 2 the hull is unstable at the speeds it reaches and the top falls again, so 0.75–2 is the usable ladder.

**`craftAtClass(spec, class)` in `engine/game/defs/craft.ts` is the ONE place the class is applied**, and it applies it by returning a SPEC: a taller impeller, the engine grown to swing it, and the `topSpeed`, `powerKw` and `accel0to50` the result actually has. Everything downstream — `topSpeedOf`, `jetCeiling`, the pump, the bot, the HUD's dial, the craft card's spec sheet — reads that spec and knows nothing about a class, which is what makes it impossible for two of them to disagree. `createGame` calls it once, from `options.speedClass`. The CATALOG's numbers stay the hull's own AT CLASS 1 — what separates the four must not move when a class does — and `tests/craft_test.ts` holds the physics to them times the class.

**And the CONTROLS are told about it** (`TUNING.pump.classSteer`). Every steering term in the model is quoted against the water in absolute metres a second — the nozzle's side force is a share of a thrust that grew with the class, the keel's bite and the carve go as v², the flat plate in the air goes as v² — so a class that scaled only the pump hands the rider a different craft rather than a faster one.

**A class is a speed, not a handling package.** The rule the dial is sized to is that one craft's classes all manoeuvre the same, and that a faster class never comes round SHARPER per metre of track than a slower one: nothing that goes faster turns tighter. A little wider is right and expected. What separates the four HULLS is untouched either way — the dial scales every craft's own numbers by the same factor, so the roster keeps its spread, and at class 1 the factor is exactly 1.

The rider's two DEFLECTIONS — the nozzle's angle and his authority in the air — are scaled by `class^-classSteer` inside `craftAtClass`, so every reader still sees one spec. Measured over 0.75 → 1.50, holding full lock from 85 % of each class's own top speed and holding the lean back off a 0.35 rad ramp, at the dial's 0 and at its shipped **¾**:

| craft  | turn °/10 m at 0 | at ¾        | peak yaw °/s at 0 | at ¾     | air pitch °/s at 0 | at ¾      |
| ------ | ---------------- | ----------- | ----------------- | -------- | ------------------ | --------- |
| skiff  | 10.8 → 15.1      | 12.5 → 11.6 | 33 → 132          | 39 → 92  | 188 → 262          | 207 → 171 |
| marlin | 7.3 → 10.7       | 8.4 → 8.2   | 36 → 123          | 42 → 116 | 139 → 152          | 151 → 139 |
| otter  | 8.9 → 10.9       | 10.4 → 8.6  | 27 → 93           | 33 → 61  | 123 → 136          | 136 → 123 |

At 0 every hull turns sharper the faster it is ridden and gives four times the yaw acceleration off the same flick of the bars — the twitch a rider reports as "the fast class is easier to crash". At ¾ the turn per metre is flat to slightly wider. What the dial does NOT flatten is the total a jump rotates through: a faster class leaves the lip harder and hangs half again as long, and taking that back would be taking the jump itself back. `tests/craft_test.ts` holds both ratios across the band.

**The dart is the one the dial cannot reach**, and it is not the dial's doing: the stand-up's turn per metre already fell across the band at 0 (13.4 → 11.0). Benched at class 1.50 it rolls to 25° under sustained full lock, its wetted share collapses to 0.05 and the yaw rate goes negative — the hull is leaving the water rather than running out of nozzle. That is the catalog's to answer, not a deflection's.

**And the course is drawn to it.** A level's gates are spaced in METRES, so a class ridden on a stock course does not stretch the race — it only gives the rider less time between gates, and measured over four seeds and the whole roster at 1.5 the gates taken fell from 139 to 110 while the gates MISSED rose from 101 to 130, because a hull that overshoots a gate has to come back for it. R32 is the answer: `generateLevel` takes the class as its `pace` and `rulesAtPace` stretches every rule number that is really a time, so a race lasts the same time at any class. With the course paced to match, the same measurement reads **140 taken, 104 missed** — stock's own race, ridden faster over more ground. A level carries the class it was drawn to as `Level.pace`.

`make level` and `make analyze` both take `PACE=` so a class can be inspected on its own terms; `make level SEED=7 PACE=1.5` quotes the stretched run-up and the classed hulls' launch speeds in its table.

## Slowing down, and what it is measured against

A watercraft has two ways of losing speed and no third: let the throttle go and let the water have it, or drop the gate. Both are benched flat — a calm sea, no wind, from a steady top speed, the bare physics (`assist: 0`) — and both are quoted against published measurements of the real thing, because "does it feel heavy enough" is a question with an answer.

The real numbers. Coasting down, an instrumented PWC averages **0.14 g to 0.31 g** over a full stop, with typical stopping distances of 125–160 ft at 30 mph and 180–225 ft at 40 mph (SAE 2005-01-1176, _Stopping Distance and Acceleration Performance of Personal Watercraft_) — deceleration the paper itself compares to a road vehicle on snow and ice. A hull on the plane does most of that early, as it comes off the plane and settles into displacement mode; the tail at walking pace is very long, and a jet ski glides a remarkably long way on the last of its speed. A craft with a modern electronic gate stops roughly **half** the distance, which manufacturers quote as "cuts your stopping distance in half" and independent tests as a hundred feet sooner at 50 mph.

What the game does, at class 1, measured from a steady top speed to 10 km/h:

| craft  | v₀ km/h | coast: g at 1 s · to half · avg g | brake: g at 1 s · to half · stop · avg g   |
| ------ | ------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| skiff  | 96      | 0.41 · 4.2 s, 81 m · 0.13         | 0.67 · 2.2 s, 43 m · 4.9 s, 64 m · 0.50    |
| marlin | 110     | 0.40 · 5.1 s, 113 m · 0.12        | 0.59 · 3.1 s, 69 m · 7.7 s, 108 m · 0.37   |
| otter  | 92      | 0.33 · 5.0 s, 92 m · 0.10         | 0.60 · 2.4 s, 44 m · 5.0 s, 65 m · 0.46    |
| dart   | 80      | 0.40 · 3.6 s, 58 m · 0.16         | — (no gate fitted; the brake IS the coast) |

The coasting column sits inside the measured band by construction and keeps the real SHAPE: a hard first second while the hull comes off the plane, then a long v² tail. The braked column lands at about the real electronic gate's half-a-g, and the marlin is the roster's weakest brake on purpose — the heaviest hull with the smallest gate on it. The braked column is the JAB's: before the gate's throttling half was driven, the same bench read skiff 0.61 · 2.3 s, 46 m · 5.0 s, 67 m, marlin 0.52 · 3.2 s, 73 m · 7.8 s, 111 m and otter 0.56 · 2.4 s, 46 m · 5.1 s, 67 m — the first second is where a gate that no longer spends it arriving shows, and the stop follows it down.

**The off-throttle steering loss is kept, and the BRAKE is the tool that answers it.** Full lock held from top speed on flat water, degrees of heading turned in four seconds, and — the honest measure when the thumb is also braking — the metres of water it takes to swing the bow through a right angle:

| craft  | gas: ° in 4 s · m to 90° | gas released: ° · m to 90° | brake: ° in 4 s · m to 90° |
| ------ | ------------------------ | -------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| skiff  | 129 · 68 m               | 21 · 169 m                 | 260 · 35 m                 |
| marlin | 112 · 88 m               | 39 · 216 m                 | 117 · 69 m                 |
| otter  | 107 · 79 m               | 13 · 209 m                 | 205 · 42 m                 |
| dart   | 159 · 48 m               | 48 · 85 m                  | 48 · 85 m (no gate)        |

The released column's `m to 90°` is read over the whole run rather than cut at the four seconds beside it: a coasting hull does come round eventually, over a hundred and seventy metres to two hundred and sixteen of water it no longer has. The braked column is the JAB's here too — before the throw it read skiff 226 · 40 m, marlin 121 · 68 m and otter 203 · 43 m on the same bench, so a gate that is down when the bars go over is worth a few metres of the corner and a good deal more heading inside the same four seconds.

Letting go of the throttle is the WORST thing a rider can do to a corner — a released thumb will not bring any of the three gated hulls round a right angle inside the four seconds the first two columns are read over, and takes two to five times the water of the throttle to do it at all — which is the real characteristic (it is the hazard off-throttle assisted steering exists to answer) and is stated again below. The gate is the one hand that scrubs speed and keeps the nozzle flowing at the same time, and two terms make it the tightest line on the craft rather than the loosest: `pump.brakeSteer` = 0.35, which is how much more everything the nozzle is worth buys with the gate fully down, and `hull.brakeBite` = 0.7, the floor the gate puts under `planing` in the carve. Both exist because the gate BURIES THE BOW: a planing hull rides on a hand's breadth of bottom aft with its forefoot clear of the water, and a braked one has its stern squatted and its forefoot and the front half of its keel in the sea. There is more hull in the water under the brake, not less, and it is further forward — which is why brake-and-turn is a thing a rider does, and why the carve reading `planing` alone was wrong about a braked hull. So the line into a buoy at speed is brake, then gas: it comes round in a little over half the water, and in less time, than the same corner taken on the throttle. The stand-up is the control — no gate fitted, so its brake key is a closed throttle and its braked corner IS its coasting one.

## Steering, and the off-throttle characteristic

- **The nozzle** swings toward the steer input at `nozzleRate` = 6 rad/s × its full deflection (a cable and a hand), to `±nozzleAngle` — the spec's, 20–26°. The thrust vector yaws with it; the yaw moment is the reaction's lever about the centre of gravity.
- **With the throttle shut** there is only the little the keel and the sponsons turn by the nozzle's attitude rather than its thrust: `pump.keelYaw` = 0.6 N·m per rad of nozzle per (m/s)² through the water, scaled by how wet the hull is. This is the real off-throttle characteristic, kept on purpose: a jet ski steers by pointing its thrust, and with no thrust there is nothing to point.
- **The high-speed steer**, an arcade dial and the one number here that is not a model: a turn rate is the lateral acceleration over the speed, so the same nozzle force swings the same hull half as fast at twice the speed, and an honest model stops answering the bars exactly where a course needs it most. `pump.steerHighSpeed` = 0.35 is how much more everything the nozzle is worth — the jet's side thrust and `keelYaw` alike — buys at the craft's OWN top speed (`topSpeedOf`), ramping in with the square of the speed so the bottom half of the range barely moves. Measured at 0.95 of top speed it is worth about a tenth more turn for a tenth off the radius, and it is sub-linear past there: most of the yaw at those speeds is already the hull's own. What holds it at 0.35 rather than higher is `make sim` over eight seeds — every craft's pace rises and the resets halve, and at 0.45 the bot starts grounding instead.
- **The carve.** A banked V bottom is a rudder: the yaw moment is `hull.carve` = 16 N·m per (m/s)² of speed through the water, read through `½·sin(2·bank)` so it peaks at 45° of bank (a hull rolled further is not a hull turning faster; the bank is clamped at ±0.8 rad), scaled by how wet the hull is (`min(1, 2·wetted)`) and by the `planing` readout. `bank` is the roll angle past a dead band: nothing at all inside `carveDead` = 0.09 rad (about 5°) of roll, where both chines are dry — the couple of degrees a crosswind heels a hull or chop rocks it are not a rudder — and only the roll beyond it counts, toward the side the hull is rolled to. This is what lets a leaned hull keep turning once the thrust — and so the nozzle's authority — has fallen away at speed, and why the stand-up (which lays over furthest) is the one craft that still turns much with the throttle shut.
- **The sponsons and the ride plate** are the two per-craft handling knobs, and they pull against each other. `sponsonBite` (0.9–1.1, 1 = the reference sponson) scales BOTH the carve's yaw moment and the sponsons' bank-in, so a craft set to bite hooks into a turn and holds it; `ridePlate` (0.78–1.1, 1 = the reference plate) scales the water's yaw damping outright and half of its pitch damping, so a long plate tracks straight and lands flat and a short one pivots. Measured on the drag strip at full lock from 20 m/s, the roster's tightest radius runs skiff 31 m, marlin 34, otter 42, dart 23.
- **The bank.** The lateral force acts on the keel below the centre of gravity, which alone would lean the hull OUT of a turn. Three things turn that round: the outside sponson planing on the water it is being pushed across (`sponsonLever` = 1.05 in units of the keel's own depth below the centre of gravity — at 1 it exactly cancels the keel's lever, and the craft's own `sponsonBite` scales it), the V bottom's own bank-in (`chineBank`), and the rider hanging off (`rider.leanIn`). `tests/craft_test.ts` holds every craft banking INTO a full-lock turn at 20 m/s, under 0.9 rad for the sit-downs and under 1.6 for the stand-up.

Measured on the synthetic drag strip — four seconds of full lock at 20 m/s, the way `craft_test` does it — the heading turned WITH the throttle open against SHUT, at the current numbers: skiff 2.41 vs 0.40 rad, marlin 2.21 vs 0.25, otter 1.94 vs 0.23, dart 2.81 vs 0.85 (the stand-up's rider steers it by leaning, and a leaned V bottom carves whether the pump is pushing or not). The test holds the ratio above 1.8 for the sit-downs and 1.2 for the dart, the shut-throttle turn under 1.2 rad, and the tightest radius under power between 4 and 80 m — 120 m for the otter, which is the slowest to come round by design. It holds a second floor at the TOP of each craft's range, where the high-speed steer is what is being judged: full lock from 0.95 of top speed comes round more than 1.2 rad in four seconds, at a radius under 48 m (54 for the otter).

Held to a steady full-lock turn at full throttle on flat water — entered at 0.9 of top speed and left to settle for six seconds — the roster averages: skiff 31 m radius at 70 km/h (1.24 g, 5.0 s to come round 180°), marlin 38 m at 79 km/h (1.30 g, 5.4 s), otter 39 m at 70 km/h (0.97 g, 6.4 s), dart 21 m at 58 km/h (1.26 g, 4.1 s). The spread across the roster is the two handling knobs above: the stand-up bites hardest and has the least plate under it, the musclecraft and the tourer the reverse. A hard carve settles a fifth below the craft's straight-line top speed, which is the cost the rider pays for the corner. That is within reach of what a ridden personal watercraft holds — a committed carve is nearer a g and a half — and what is left of the gap is the BOT rather than the water: the loop in `sim/bot.ts` weaves rather than holding a line once the hull comes round faster than it can aim, and `simulation_test`'s synthetic-shore cases go red. Raising `carve` further means re-deriving that loop in the same change.

## The air (`flight.ts`)

Afloat, the air is drag against the WIND-relative velocity (`windAt` at the craft's height — the gusts, the eddy field, the log-law profile), `ρ_air` = `air.density` = 1.225 kg/m³. It is resolved in the HULL'S OWN AXES, because the air does not meet a craft equally from every side and does not push equally along it:

- **Nose-on and vertically**, `½·ρ_air·C_dA·|v_rel|·v_rel` with the spec's frontal `cdA` (hull plus rider, 0.70–0.95 m²) — unchanged, which is why nothing about a top speed or a flight moved when the beam got its own answer.
- **On the beam**, that same frontal term PLUS the extra area of the side profile. A craft head-on is a beam wide by a foot of topside; the same craft abeam is a slab most of its length by most of its freeboard with a bluff body's coefficient behind it, which is what `cdASide` = 1.6–2.4 m² carries. The extra is applied by the **crossflow principle** (Hoerner 1965 §3): the force across a slender body goes with the square of the CROSSFLOW alone, not with the whole airspeed times it. At rest the only airflow there is IS crossflow, so the hull feels the whole slab; at ninety km/h the sideslip is a few degrees and almost none of it is in the flow. Both ends have to be right, and taking the slab at speed instead is worth some hundreds of newtons a hull has no business feeling — measured, it moved the bot's pace across the roster by a fifth, in both directions.

The two act in **different places**. The fore-and-aft push is on the centreline at `flight.windageY` = 0.3 m above the centre of gravity and `flight.windageZ` = −0.12 of the length (aft), where the rider sits, and turns nothing. The sideways one is at `flight.windageSideZ` = +0.08 of the length, the centroid of the above-water side profile: take that profile as a trapezoid, a quarter of a metre of freeboard at the transom and two thirds of one at the stem, and its centroid `(h₁ + 2h₂)/3(h₁ + h₂)` sits 0.58 of the length from the transom — 8 % of it forward of amidships, pulled back a little by the rider and pushed out again by where the centre of gravity is.

**The sign is the whole point**, and it is the one thing here that is not a guess. The wet hull's lateral centre is right aft (`hull.lateralStationShare` puts a third of the sideways push on the transom station alone: the pump tunnel, the ride plate, the sponsons) and the air's stands forward of it. Wind pressure ahead of water resistance is a body that will NOT nose up into the wind the way a moored boat on a chain does; it lies across it and blows away.

**And the whole of it can be checked against a measurement.** The search-and-rescue _leeway_ field experiments drift real objects at sea and quote what each makes as a share of the 10 m wind: **a personal watercraft with one person aboard comes out at 4.24 %**, with divergence angles — the angle between the drift and straight downwind — running out to forty-odd degrees for small craft. At neutral, the model gives **4.0–5.3 %** across the roster at riding wind speeds, and lies 37° off downwind at 6 m/s squaring to 4° at 16. Nothing is aimed at those numbers: they are what the two centres and the wet hull produce. `tests/flight_test.ts` holds the whole roster inside the band, which is what sizes every `cdASide`; it subtracts the sampled water transport so the result is wind leeway rather than Stokes drift or current.

Out of the water — `airShare` = `1 − 3·wetted` clipped to 0..1, and 1 when airborne — three more things fade in:

- **The flat plate.** The hull at angle of attack `α` to the airflow with the Newtonian normal-force coefficient `C_N = 2·sin α·cos α` (Hoerner 1965; good to 45° and bounded past it) on a plate of `plateShare` = 0.55 of `length × beam`, acting `cpLead` = 0.08 of the length ahead of the centre of gravity: a nose-up hull in a headwind lifts its nose further — the flat plate's static instability, and what a rider leans against.
- **The rider's authority**, stated as the arcade number it is: `leanTorque` = 450 N·m at full lean in pitch (nose-up for lean back), `steerRoll` = 140 N·m at full steer in roll and `steerYaw` = 60 N·m in yaw — every one of them times the craft's own `riderAuthority` (0.85–1.5, 1 = a seated rider on a runabout), because a rider standing on a 150 kg stand-up with their whole mass free to move commands far more of it than one sat behind a backrest. That number is most of what separates a freestyle machine from a boat, and `make sim`'s DIVE column is where it shows: it is the authority to LEVEL a hull for its landing, and cutting the tourer's to 0.7 in an early pass put it from 7 dives over four seeds to 3 at 0.9 (measured). Real riders do rotate a craft in the air by pulling on the bars and moving their mass; the size is chosen for what the air game needs rather than measured — the hold alone is sized for ATTITUDE: a lean held forward through a 0.7 s hang puts the nose 20–30° down, not on the water's floor, and a normal jump levels with a touch. **THE PUMP** is what a backflip is made of, and it is a COMMITTED impulse rather than a torque. It is read off the SHAPE of the lean-back input rather than its value (`craft.ts`, `TUNING.flight.pump*`), which is how one control does both of the things a rider does with it:

- A **haul** is earned every time the lean-back input is carried across `flight.pumpGate` = 0.8 — a GATE up at the top of the axis, not a dead band near the bottom of it. Below it a lean is TRIM and buys the hold's own torque and nothing else; at or past it, every fresh crossing is one whole haul. A key HELD down is one haul however long it is held — the crossing it made is never un-made while it is held — and a key WORKED is one haul a tap. **Only MAXING the lean is a trick**, which is the whole reason the gate is where it is: a rider crossing a real sea trims constantly, a touch back over a crest and a touch forward down its face, and a threshold set anywhere a trim lives cannot tell the two apart.
- **What the flight STARTS from is the deck's or the water's**, and it is the difference between setting a trick up and riding. Every step the hull has something under it rearms the mark: on a **ramp's deck** it is zeroed, so the lean held back up the deck reads as one whole rise at the lip and is paid there; on the **water** it is set to the lean the rider is already carrying, so the same hold as a crest drops out from under him is no rise at all and buys nothing. A flight then leaves the mark alone from its first step to its last, including the `minAir` before it counts as one — otherwise a rider's own throw would be taken back off him a fifth of a second after he made it. Out on the water a stroke is something he STARTS: a tap, or the bars pushed further than he was carrying them, once the hull is already up. Measured before the rule landed, holding one turn across an Hs 3 m sea for thirty seconds: 3–6 throws earned and 0.98 of a barrel roll off a crest nobody jumped, with the landing assist folded away by the same stroke — a craft that spins when the rider leaves a wave.
- A haul is ALL OR NOTHING: every crossing is the whole of `flight.pump` = 620 N·m·s, and everything short of the gate is nothing at all. That is what keeps the levelling control out of the trick, and it is why the app's lean keys ramp as fast as they do (`KEY_LEAN_ATTACK` = 18 / `KEY_LEAN_RELEASE` = 16 in `input-model.ts`): a key can only reach the top of an axis by being held there, so a soft ramp would have left a keyboard rider unable to tap a flip out at all. At that rate a press of 0.1 s reaches 0.86 and buys a haul, a flick of 0.05 s reaches 0.62 and trims, and a hand working the key above about six a second stops clearing the gate. On touch the same end of the same axis is the edge of the handlebar's reach ring (`BAR_REACH_PX`), so the circle the player can see is where the trick starts.
- It is SPENT once the hull is flying: off the water going up (`flight.launchVy`), with the deck behind it, and `flight.minAir` past that. Let the lean go before then and the haul goes with it. Each one subtracts `pump · riderAuthority / I_x` from the pitch rate, nose-up; pulling only, because a rider standing on the hull has nothing to push the nose down against.
- **A TRICK CANNOT BE OPENED ON THE WAY DOWN.** The FIRST stroke of a flight — either kind — is only spent by a hull whose `vy` is still positive, because a rider leaning back as the water comes up is a rider reaching for his landing and must not be handed a flip for it. `launchVy` says the flight BEGAN going up, which a flight past its apex still satisfies; this is the reading at the moment he asks. Once he has committed to one going up he IS committed (`CraftState.tricking`), and every stroke after the first is his to throw whichever way the hull is going — so a flip opened off the lip is worked the whole way down to the water, and a roll can be thrown into it on the way. The latch is cleared the moment the water or a deck has the hull again.
- The bound is the FLIGHT'S BUDGET, not a count of taps: `flight.pumpCeiling` = 8 rad/s times the craft's `riderAuthority` is what one rider has to give one jump (`CraftState.pumped`), and the hull is back to a fresh one the moment the water or a deck has it. `flight.pumpReady` puts a floor of about an eighth of a second between hauls — nothing faster than a hand can count.
- A haul also throws the rider BACK: `flight.yankReach` = 0.3 m on top of `rider.leanReach` for `yankFade` = 0.45 s (`CraftState.yank` → `riderAft`), which is what the player sees when the taps land and a real nose-up couple off his weight while it lasts. The same fading yank stands in for the lean in the air's HOLD and in what folds the landing assist away, so a tapped flip has the hold behind it between the taps and the arcade's hand does not come back in the gaps.

How many taps a flip costs is the craft's own and no knob of its own: `pump · riderAuthority / I_x` runs 1.1 rad/s on the tourer to 5.7 on the stand-up. Measured on an 18° ramp — the middle of the band R8 builds — with the lean held back up the deck and then worked at five taps a second: the stand-up comes round 2.0 turns on the hold alone and 2.5 on the taps, the runabout 1.0 and 1.6, the musclecraft 0.6 and 1.3, and the tourer 0.5 and 1.0 — which is the trick going from out of reach on two of the four hulls to within reach on all of them. `airPitchTorque(spec)` in `limits.ts` is the hold's number for the bot, this craft's rider included; the pump is scaled by the same number, and **the bot never hauls** (`sim/bot.ts` caps its nose-up air lean at its own `airLeanCap` = 0.22, far under the gate: a levelling loop that hauled would be a bot flipping by accident. The cap is the LOOP's number rather than the gate's, and that distinction was measured — capping at the gate instead handed a PD tuned against 0.22 four times the authority, and it stood its own hull on its tail to trim a landing: seed 1 took a fourth reset and seed 3 four more).

### The two strokes (`strokes.ts`)

**THE WHIP is the pump on the other axis**, and the two are one mechanism read twice — which is why they live together in `engine/game/strokes.ts` rather than in the craft's step. Where the pump is the bars hauled BACK and buys rotation nose-over-tail, the whip is the bars thrown OVER and buys rotation about the hull's own length: the SIDE SPIN, which is the one trick a rider can ask for with the hand he is already steering with. Every clause above holds on it unchanged — a crossing of `flight.whipGate` = 0.85 earns a throw and everything short of it is just steering, a held key earns exactly one off a deck and none at all off a crest, a worked key earns one a tap wherever the hull left from, every throw is the whole impulse, nothing is spent until the hull is FLYING, no FIRST throw is spent by a hull on the way down, and `flight.whipCeiling` = 12 rad/s times `riderAuthority` is the budget one rider has to give one flight.

Three things are the whip's alone:

- **It has a SIDE.** The bars crossing the centre end the throw that was running and start a fresh one on the new side, because a rider who has thrown his weight the other way is not a rider whose weight is still out there. The budget (`CraftState.whipped`) is unsigned, so throwing one way and then the other spends one budget and not two — a rider can stop a roll he has started, out of what is left.
- **Its gate is higher than the pump's** (0.85 against 0.8). The bars are in the rider's hands the whole way down every straight and through every gate, so a sideways throw has to be unmistakably a throw and not the lock he was already carrying: full lock is a corner, and a hair past full lock is a side spin.
- **Its impulse is the same number as the pump's** (`flight.whip` = 620 N·m·s), which looks like laziness and is a measurement. A hull's roll inertia is about a quarter of its pitch inertia, so the same impulse thrown sideways starts four times the rate — but `rotDamp` is one coefficient over all three axes, and over a quarter of the inertia it bleeds a roll away about four times as fast (0.53/s against pitch's 0.14/s on the skiff at 18 m/s). What is easy to start is hard to keep, and the two cancel.

So it is set by the ladder it makes, measured with `make ride SCENARIO=sidespin` on the first ramp of seed 38 — the real article at 15–22° and 1.4–1.7 s of air — in revolutions turned with the bars held over, and the flip off the same ramp beside it for scale:

| Craft  | Roll, held | Flip, held |
| ------ | ---------- | ---------- |
| skiff  | 1.76       | 0.89       |
| marlin | 1.04       | 0.43       |
| otter  | 0.91       | 0.40       |
| dart   | 1.61       | 1.78       |

Hold the bars over and it comes round on three of the four; the tourer is the one that has to be WORKED, and a worked throw is now a deeper move than it was. What the gate costs is measured on the keyboard's own steer ramp (`KEY_STEER_ATTACK` = 6, left alone because steering is the control the whole run is ridden on and an instant one is not a steering feel): a press has to last about a third of a second to carry the axis past 0.85, so the bars are worked at about 1.5 a second rather than four, and presses shorter than that buy no throw at all. On TOUCH there is no ramp between the thumb and the axis, so a throw is as quick as the hand: over to the ring, off, and over again. **The bot never throws either** — `sim/bot.ts` caps its air steer at its own `airBarsCap` = 0.3 the way it caps its nose-up lean at `airLeanCap` — but only on a REAL launch, read off `CraftState.launchVy`. Two things were measured into that. Capping through the chop as well took the bars off its levelling loop for every crest it skips off, and the stand-up lost two gates of six on the synthetic shore. And quoting the engine's own `minAir` clause in the bot leaves one step on which the bot has not capped and the engine already counts the hull as flying — one step was a whole throw, because a flight off a RAMP starts its mark at zero, and it cost seven accidental barrel rolls over sixteen runs. Off the launch instead, those same sixteen runs turn zero rolls and zero flips by accident. The rearming rule above has since taken the same trap out of every flight off the WATER, which is why it moves not one digest of `make sim`: the bot was already under both thresholds everywhere the rule now applies.

- **Rotational damping** `rotDamp` = 35 N·m·s at `rotDampSpeed` = 20 m/s, scaling with airspeed (floored at 5 m/s), so a flight nobody is steering does not tumble.

## The water over the deck (`submerged.ts`)

`airShare` above is one end of a crossing, and the other end used to be nothing: the moment the hull was wet enough (`wetted` past a third) `flight.ts` returned, and the rider's lean, roll and yaw went to zero. Afloat that is right — his weight shift, the nozzle and, stood up, `stand.hoist` are what he steers a floating hull with. Driven UNDER it is not: a submerged rider still has the bars, and he now has them in water eight hundred times denser than the air he had a moment ago. Riders hold a watercraft under for seconds at a time and ride it tens of metres, trading depth against throttle — too little and it corks straight back out, too much and the nose keeps going down.

**The regime** is `submergedShare(bottomUnder, deckFill)`, the product of two readings `hull.ts` takes in its first pass: `bottomUnder`, the LEAST-immersed bottom probe's fill, and `deckFill`, the mean fill of the four deck probes. Both are needed, because each alone confuses two cases that must not be confused — `bottomUnder` alone calls a bow driven in with a dry transom "under" (ordinary head-sea work, and no business of this module's), and `deckFill` alone says the same of a hull floating INVERTED, whose deck is awash and whose bottom is in the air. A depth in metres cannot do the job at all: `hull.submerged` is the DEEPEST probe, so it reads a buried bow as a submerged hull and cannot see the thing that matters, whether the hull as a whole has left the surface. Measured over forty seconds at every wind the generator deals, the product is **exactly zero on flat water** on all four craft and engages in 1–10 % of steps in a head sea, at the 99th percentile and above — green water over the deck, and nowhere else.

**What the rider commands in it**, every dial an angular ACCELERATION at a full input and a full share, the way `stand.hoist` is and for the same reason (the roster's inertia spans three times over, and what the number describes is the rider rather than any one hull): `submerged.lean` = 3.8 rad/s² of nose-up on a lean back, `submerged.roll` = 2.4 and `submerged.yaw` = 0.6 — the yaw modest because the nozzle is still working down there and owns most of it. All three times the craft's own `riderAuthority`, so the stand-up (1.5) rides out of a bury the tourer (0.85) drowns in with no knob of its own, and all three shrink with the tuck exactly as the air's do.

**The engine does not cut.** The throttle's tilt cut-off stands in for the rider having come off and taken the lanyard with him, which is what actually stops a watercraft's engine; a hull under the water is a rider mid-manoeuvre with his intake fed better than it has ever been, so `onFeet` is `up.y > 0` **or** under. The capsize gets no such exemption and needs none: a bury keeps its deck up (`up.y` stays above 0.9 through the whole of it, measured over the roster on the air gate's own dive), so a dive never started that clock — what reaches `up.y < 0` is a hull that has been rolled, and that is over however deep it is.

**The deck floods.** `hull.deckShare` = 0.55 of the bottom's displacement is the sealed volume above it, and it is there so a hull heeled onto its gunwale is righted by it and one all the way over still floats. For those it is right — a capsized hull traps its air under an upturned deck. For a hull driven UNDER the same volume is a fiction: the footwells are open to the sea, the seat is a cushion and the engine bay is a hatch rather than a tank. So `floodedDeck` takes it down to `submerged.deckSealed` = 0.3 of itself over the regime, which is per-craft for free — the volume is a fraction of each hull's own `displacement` against each hull's own mass, so the stand-up corks out at 2.4 times its weight where the tourer does it at 3.3. It is gated on the regime and not on attitude, so at the surface the share is 0 and every deck probe carries its whole volume, which is what the capsize rests on.

**What this does NOT do** is make a watercraft submarine on demand. The hull floats awash: driven in at pace with the throttle open, its keel reaches about 0.7 m down and stays there whatever the rider asks for, because the buoyancy of a fully immersed hull is 2.4–3.3 times its weight and the jet can only point down by pointing the hull down, which lifts the transom clear. That is the real machine's behaviour too — a submarine is a professional freestyle trick, not a manoeuvre — and going deeper than it would mean cutting the hulls' buoyancy well below what Archimedes gives on their stated displacement: a designed arcade override rather than a correction, and not one made here.

### The tuck (`CraftInput.crouch`, `TUNING.tuck`)

The rider can get down behind the bars — a hold, on SHIFT, and a keyboard control only (a touch rider has two thumbs and both are already on the bar and the lever). `CraftInput.crouch` is 0..1 analogue; the engine lags it into `CraftState.crouch` at `tuck.lag` = 0.22 s, because getting down and back up is a body rather than a switch, and everything below reads that lagged number and never the input. A reset or a capsize stands him back up.

**What it buys.** `tuck.dragCut` = 0.18 comes off `cdA`, and here is where the 18 % comes from. `cdA` is hull plus rider in roughly even shares: a runabout's deck, screen and bow present something like 0.4 m² of drag area, and a rider sat up behind them about 0.35 m² — roughly 0.35 m² of shoulders and head at a bluff-body `C_d` near 1. Dropping the head and shoulders behind the bars takes about 40 % off the RIDER's half, which is 18 % of the pair. The cross-check is cycling, where the same measurement is made often: a road rider on the hoods sits at `C_dA` 0.35–0.40 m² and in the drops at 0.28–0.32, and since the bicycle is a small part of that, the rider-alone reduction is near 30 % for a change of posture less committed than this one. It also lowers where the air pushes: `tuck.windageLeft` = 0.6 of `flight.windageY`, since what is still up there is mostly hull.

**And what that is worth, which is not much, and honestly so.** A personal watercraft is stopped by the WATER: at the top of the skiff's range the aero term is some 320 N against a total the pump is holding up in the thousands. Benched flat and calm (the outermost point of seed 38, sea flattened, full throttle, entered along the offshore contour), the roster gains **0.7–1.1 km/h** at the ceiling and **nothing measurable off the line** — 0–50 km/h moves by a hundredth of a second, because at 50 km/h the air is a few per cent of the drag and the hump is all of it.

What makes it a decision is the head wind, because drag goes as the CLOSING speed squared. The same 18 % on the same skiff:

| Head wind | Top, sat up | Top, tucked | Gain | 70 → 90 km/h, sat up | ...tucked |
| --------- | ----------- | ----------- | ---- | -------------------- | --------- |
| calm      | 95.5 km/h   | 96.5        | +1.0 | 3.44 s               | 3.18 s    |
| 8 m/s     | 92.7        | 94.1        | +1.4 | 4.90 s               | 4.05 s    |
| 14 m/s    | 90.3        | 92.0        | +1.7 | 29.35 s              | 13.81 s   |

The tuck is a headwind tool, which is what it is in life too.

**What it costs**, and none of it is a tax — it is the same body. A man folded down behind the bars cannot slide back down the seat, hang off the side, sweep the bars through their full throw, or throw his mass about in the air:

- `tuck.leanCut` = 0.42 off the rider's weight shift, `rider.leanReach` and `rider.leanIn` both. This is most of the cost, because hanging off IS the turn on a watercraft.
- `tuck.lockLeft` = 0.82 of the steering lock still asked for. The nozzle's own maximum (`limits.ts`) does not move; the reach does.
- `tuck.airLeft` = 0.45 of `riderAuthority` in the air — the hold, the roll and the yaw. Landing a jump tucked is a bad idea, on purpose.

Benched at a matched entry speed (re-staged at the outer point, 60 km/h, two seconds straight, then full lock — matched because radius goes as `v²` and the tucked craft has the higher ceiling), the tightest radius grows: skiff 35.1 → 44.5 m, marlin 40.6 → 47.6, otter 42.8 → 50.8, dart 26.8 → 43.7. **The stand-up pays most**, and that is the design rather than an accident: the dart is steered by a man moving his whole mass about, so folding him up takes more of its turn than any other hull's — tuck it and you have thrown away the thing you bought it for. `tests/tuck_test.ts` holds both halves of the bargain, the size of the speed half, the ordering of the cost, and the lag.

## The stand-up (`CraftState.stand`)

The tuck's mirror, and the other half of the rider's body: off the seat, up on his feet and back over the transom, standing the craft on **its tail**. It is asked for the way a rider asks for it — the lean held back with the throttle open, both at once — and nothing about it is a new force on the hull. It is the same point mass `rider.leanReach` already moves, moved further and higher, plus the one thing a rider afloat did not have and plainly does in life: something to push against.

- `stand.reach` = 0.68 m further aft than the seated lean reaches, so a stood rider is about 1.23 m behind neutral.
- `stand.rise` = 0.42 m higher than `riderHeight`. **This is the number that makes it a trick rather than a position**: a centre of gravity that far up has less righting left the further the hull rears, while the jet's nose-up couple under it does not shrink at all.
- `stand.hoist` = 4.6 rad/s² of nose-up at a full stand, faded out by `cos(pitch)` — the rider's legs and back against the bars with his feet planted. Stated as an ACCELERATION, not a torque, because the roster's pitch inertia runs 165 to 490 kg·m²: one dial, the same correction on every hull. It is the arcade twin of `flight.leanTorque`, and it exists because the pair was the wrong way round — a rider could haul the nose up with 450 N·m in mid-air and had nothing but his weight on the water, where he actually has something to push against.

**Getting up and staying up are different asks**, and every bar is lower for the second (`stand.keep` = 0.55 of each):

- `stand.dwell` = 0.9 s of the lean and the throttle both held before he commits at all. This is what separates the trick from the ride: leaning back is ordinary technique — up a ramp, through a head sea, off a crest — and every one of those is a BURST, while standing up is a HOLD. Without it the two are the same input and the trick fires by accident; the bot's ramp lean-backs alone put the marlin on the beach nine times over four seeds.
- `stand.throttle` = 0.55 and `stand.lean` = 0.72 to commit. Balancing on the tail IS throttle work, so with one bar for both, every correction the rider made dropped him back onto the seat and the dwell started again from nothing — the trick could be entered and never held.
- `stand.wetted` = 0.18 of the bottom in the water to stand up FROM, gating the START only. A hull he has already stood up has its bottom out of the water by definition, so holding him to it throughout put him back on the seat at 27° of the 77° he was on his way to.

**Overdoing it puts him over the back, and that falls out rather than being written.** The rider's weight moment shrinks as `cos(pitch)` and so does the hoist; the jet's couple does not. So past the balance the craft keeps going and the capsize catches it (`up.y` < 0) — too much lean, or too much throttle, and you are in the water, which is the trick's whole price. Benched from the hole shot at full commitment every craft goes right over (~90°); balanced with a crude human-grade policy (0.15 s reaction, coarse throttle) the roster holds 30°-plus for skiff 2.6 s, marlin 6.2 s, otter 1.8 s, dart 3.9 s. **Riding it on its back for a count is not scored yet** — `engine/game/tricks.ts` pays the air and the revolutions, and a hull on its tail is the same machinery with another term in it.

**A hull on the plane at speed cannot be stood up on flat water**, and that is the planing lift doing its job: it grows as `v²` and pins the bow. What opens the attitude is a WAVE, which is where the trick belongs — and a rear-up taken into a face carries into the flight, where the pull finishes what a backflip is made of.

**The bot never stands up**, for the same reason it never tucks: it is the instrument the roster is balanced on. The dwell is what guarantees it — the bot's lean-backs are bursts — and every `make sim` digest is unchanged by this control.

**The bot never tucks** (`sim/bot.ts`). It is the instrument the roster is balanced on, so a crouch it learned would fold the tuck's gain into every craft's `make sim` numbers and leave nothing to read the feature against. Every digest is unchanged by this control.

**The events.** The hull is airborne when nothing on it touches water, ground or ramp — read, not declared. A launch is reported once the hull has been clear for `flight.minAir` = 0.2 s (a stern probe re-touching a ramp's lip for a step is not two jumps) and only if it left with at least `launchVy` = 1.2 m/s of climb (a chop hop is not a jump); a landing only after a flight that long. **AIR TIME is a further line**: a flight under `flight.airCounts` = 0.5 s is not time in the air at all — the HUD's air clock does not start for it, it earns no line in the news column, the sim does not add it to the run's air, and it cannot take the record. A hull in a head sea is clear of the water about a fifth of the steps, in skips of a few hundredths each, and every one of those still lands, still slams and still throws a sheet; it just did not go anywhere. The longest flight of a run that DID count is kept as `progress.bestAir`, and the landing that took it carries `record` so a presentation can call it once. A dive develops over the steps after a landing — the bow keeps going in — and is reported once per landing when the bow probe is `diveDepth` = 0.55 m under with the nose below `divePitch` = −0.12 rad.

## The arcade's hand (`assist.ts`)

### The following sea (`followingSeaAssist`)

A head sea supplies its own answer: the bow meets the face, the rising bottom earns pressure lift, and entering probes slam upward. Running **with** the waves reverses that advantage. A crest can lift the transom under a short skip, leave the hull pointing down the back of the wave, and then overtake the bow before either physical term has a useful angle to work through. Once the section is buried, `hull.diveCd` takes the speed and the rider loses the ride. The following-sea hand is the intentionally arcade correction for that directional asymmetry.

It is a pitch-up acceleration, never a force lifting the whole craft. The hull must be making way and its horizontal nose must lie inside `assist.following.align` = 0.65 of the wave-travel direction (a 49° cone), so beam and head seas get exactly zero. While airborne it catches only a nose-down bow inside `airReach` = 1 m of the face, aiming for `landPitch` = 0.1 rad over a `pitchBand` = 0.15 rad; the rider's own air input folds it away. Once wet it starts beyond `begin` = 0.12 m of bow immersion, reaches full at `full` = 0.45 m, and requires the bow to lead the transom by up to `difference` = 0.2 m. If the deck follows under, `CraftState.submerged` — the least-immersed bottom probe times the deck fill — keeps the correction engaged until the hull breaks the surface again. A forward lean fades the hand linearly and a full forward lean cancels it: accidental stuffing is caught, but deliberately diving into the face remains the rider's. `right` = 12 rad/s² and `damp` = 4 rad/s² per rad/s are accelerations, multiplied by each craft's pitch inertia so the whole roster gets the same hand.

The acceptance case is `make ride SCENARIO=following CRAFT=otter SEED=29`, read beside `SCENARIO=chop`. Over 100 seeds and all four craft, twenty seconds each, the bare following ride kept the whole hull more than 0.1 under for at least 0.25 s in **54 of 400** cases, with the longest continuous submergence at **1.39 s**. With the hand, **0 of 400** stays under that long; the remaining water-over-deck contacts all clear inside 0.24 s. Deep bow contacts past the 0.55 m dive line fall from 76 cases to 16, while the head-sea trajectory and readings are unchanged because its alignment gate is zero. An upward lift pulse was also benched and rejected: changing the heave timing left 13 of the known worst cases under, where correcting the attitude left none.

### The air (`landingAssist`)

This is an arcade game, and the sensation it sells is the flight: the launch, the hang, and a landing ridden away from. The model above does not deliver that on its own. Thrown off a lip by a wave it met on the way up, with the flat plate winding the nose up over the whole hang, an uncorrected hull goes in badly about **two flights in three** — 68.3%, measured over a grid of 3360 staged launches: four craft, four speeds, six launch attitudes, seven roll angles, five pitch rates, with the rider's hands still. That is the sea winning an argument the rider never got to have, so the last moment before the water is the arcade's.

**One question, asked every step: where will this hull be pointing when it hits?** `timeToWater(height, vy)` is the ballistic clock — the positive root of `h + vy·t − ½g·t² = 0`, the air's drag left out because over the tenths of a second it is read across it moves the answer by percent and a closed form is what lets the attitude be predicted rather than stepped. Inside `assist.air.window` = 0.75 s of the water, the craft's own attitude is turned forward at the rate it is already turning at (`integrate(q, ω, tti)` — the same quaternion step the body is advanced with, taken in one jump), and world-up is read in that arriving hull's frame against where a hull landing well would see it: level but for `assist.air.landPitch` of bow lift. The shortest turn between the two is split into its pitch and roll parts, each read against its **own** tolerance — `assist.air.pitchTolerance` and `assist.air.rollTolerance` are far apart because the hull is: it rides away from a landing twenty degrees off in roll, and `flight.divePitch` says seven degrees of nose-down is already a dive. Past the tolerance the correction grows from zero, and again as the water closes.

Three properties are the whole design, and `tests/assist_test.ts` holds each:

- **Predicting first is what makes it flip-safe.** A hull three-quarters of the way through a rotation is a long way from level right now and a short way from it at splashdown, so the shortest correction from the PREDICTED attitude carries the rotation on round; the same spring read off the present attitude would unwind a flip into the water.
- **A flight that was going to be fine is not touched at all** — the torque comes back exactly zero, so a rider who lands his own jumps never feels it.
- **The air is the rider's before it is the arcade's.** `lean` folds the hand away in proportion, either way it is held: winding a flip round or stuffing the nose on purpose both get the bare physics. It has to be the INPUT that says so rather than the rotation, because a hull half way through a flip is genuinely predicted to land on its head and only the rider knows that is deliberate. The assist owns pitch and roll and never heading — a hand that yawed the craft would be steering it.

`assist.air.right` and `assist.air.damp` are quoted as ACCELERATIONS (rad/s² per rad of error, and per rad/s of rate) rather than torques, because the roster's pitch inertia runs from 165 to 490 kg·m² and a hand quoted in N·m would catch the dart three times as hard as the otter; `craft.ts` multiplies each axis by that craft's own inertia, so one dial means one correction on every hull.

**A chop hop is not a jump.** The assist also waits for a real flight — `airTime` past `flight.minAir`, the same line the launch event is read against. At speed in a head sea the hull is clear of the water a fifth of the steps in skips of a few hundredths each, and every one of those is inside the window and hard up against it, so without that gate the assist is a pitch damper permanently fitted to the ride. It costs the bench nothing (real flights are seconds long) and it is what keeps the drumroll: `make ride SCENARIO=chop` holds a pitch range of −10°…18° against the bare physics' −14°…33°, which is the 33° wheelie going and the rhythm staying. `launch`, `apex`, `landing`, `dive` and `backflip` all come back byte-identical to `--assist 0` at the shipped setting.

**It is TWO dials.** `GameState.assist` (0..1) is how hard the hand pulls and `GameState.assistWindow` (s) is how long before the water it arrives; `createGame({ assist, assistWindow })` sets them, `--assist` on `make ride` and `make sim` is how a before-and-after of the strength is taken, and `TUNING.assist.air.strength` / `.window` are the defaults when nothing says. The window is the one that decides whether a flight is a DECISION — the whole hang above it is the rider's alone — so a difficulty setting that wants a lighter hand shortens it as well as softening it, and `TUNING.assist.band` is the ladder — of those two and of the ramp's own hand, which comes down a rung more slowly because it is the less visible of the two:

| rung       | `none` | `light` | `half`     | `full` |
| ---------- | ------ | ------- | ---------- | ------ |
| `strength` | 0      | 0.25    | **0.5**    | 1      |
| `window`   | 0 s    | 0.5 s   | **0.75 s** | 1.1 s  |
| `ramp`     | 0      | 0.25    | **0.6**    | 1      |

Every rung is written down rather than computed, so the ladder can be re-measured rung by rung when the difficulty setting that reads it lands. The ladder, over the flight bench (3360 staged launches, four craft, the rider's hands still — the share that end in a dive, a capsize or on the hull's side):

| `assist`     | 0     | 0.25  | 0.5      | 0.75 | 1    |
| ------------ | ----- | ----- | -------- | ---- | ---- |
| bad landings | 68.3% | 13.9% | **7.7%** | 5.7% | 5.0% |

0 is the bare physics with nothing between the rider and the sea; 1 is the full arcade. The shipped default is **0.5** — the middle of its own range on purpose, because this number is about to become a difficulty scale and a default at the top of its range is a scale with one direction. It is read and never written during a run, draws no randomness, and so replays identically at any setting; `--assist 0` reproduces the pre-assist tables exactly, seed for seed and digest for digest.

**The backflip.** `tests/flight_test.ts` holds it at both ends. On a 10 m ramp at 0.5 rad — steeper than any the generator lays — entered at 20 m/s with the lean held back from the deck onward, one haul plus the hold rotates the hull through more than 2π in more than 1.5 s of air; the same ramp levelled by the rider (a PD on the pitch, which is nose-DOWN off a lip and so earns no haul at all) lands upright and rides on. And on an 18° deck 9 m long, the middle of the band R8 actually lays, the tourer comes round 0.4 of a turn on the hold and past a whole one on the taps — which is the case the pump exists for, and the test says so. The rotation is worth points and a multiplier (`tricks.ts`).

### The ramp (`rampAssist`)

The other way a jump you had already earned is taken off you, and it happens before the flight rather than at the end of it. A ramp's deck is **8 m** wide (R8's `ramp.width`, times whatever R33's dial dealt the run — 4 m is the bottom rung of it, and the width the bench below was measured on) with a hull a metre and a bit in the beam on it, and a hull on it has nothing in the water: no keel to bite, no nozzle to steer with (no intake, no thrust — see above), and `contact.rampFriction` = 0.08 under it, which is most of a skid. So whatever sideways way it climbed aboard with is the way it leaves: a hull that climbs on **a metre off the centreline and six degrees off the axis** — a line a rider would call lined up — is over the flank two metres before the lip. Measured over the ramp bench — 800 staged approaches, eight seeds and four craft, started six metres before the hinge up to half the half-width off the centreline and up to 7° off the axis, throttle open and the rider's hands still — the bare physics follows through off the lip on **78.8%** of them and skids off a flank on the rest. That bench ran on the four-metre deck, which R33 made the dial's bottom rung rather than the width R8 draws; on the eight-metre deck the bare hull keeps far more of those jumps unaided, so the figures below describe the narrow ramp and the hand is doing less of the work on the wide one.

Three terms, and the design is in what is NOT among them:

- **The deck holds.** `assist.ramp.grip` = 3 /s damps the across velocity, the way a keel in the water would. It is a damper and nothing else, so a hull tracking straight up the deck feels no force at all, anywhere on it, and one sliding off finds the slide fading rather than a wall.
- **The bow comes round.** `assist.ramp.align` = 2.2 rad/s² per rad turns the hull toward the ramp's axis past a dead band of `aim` = 0.05 rad (3°), with `damp` = 1.6 on the yaw rate about the vertical so it arrives settled rather than weaving. Leaving the lip pointing straight is most of what following through IS.
- **The camber**, and only at the edges. `assist.ramp.centre` = 2.5 m/s² is the one term that knows where the middle is, so it is kept smallest and kept OUT of the middle: nothing inside `free` = 0.4 of the half-width, growing from zero at that line to the full figure at the flank. `most` = 6 m/s² caps the two together — a hull that arrived broadside off a wave is one the deck cannot save, and a hand that tried would be a wall on the centreline.

**It must not read as a gutter**, and that is a stronger claim than "it is small". Nothing here aims a hull at the centreline from the middle of the deck, and nothing here moves a hull that is not already going somewhere: every term fades with `carry`, how fast the hull is actually travelling UP the deck against `pace` = 6 m/s. A craft parked on a ramp, sliding back down one, or crossing one broadside gets exactly nothing, however far off the middle it sits. `tests/assist_test.ts` holds all of that, including the one that matters most: a run up the deck with the bow on the axis comes back **bit-identical** at any dial.

**It is a dial, and a separate one.** `GameState.rampAssist` (0..1) is what a run's deck is ridden with, `createGame({ rampAssist })` sets it, `--ramp-assist` on `make ride` and `make sim` moves it alone, and `createGame({ assist })` moves both difficulty hands together so `--assist 0` is still the bare air-and-ramp physics everywhere. `TUNING.assist.ramp.strength` is the default when nothing says, and the `ramp` row of `TUNING.assist.band` is where a difficulty rung moves it beside the air's two. The ladder, over the ramp bench (the share that follows through off the lip):

| `rampAssist`     | 0     | 0.25  | 0.5   | 0.6       | 0.75  | 1     |
| ---------------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | --------- | ----- | ----- |
| followed through | 78.8% | 85.9% | 91.5% | **92.0%** | 93.1% | 98.3% |

The ladder flattens between 0.5 and 0.75 and then opens again: at 1 the hand is firm enough to hold a hull that climbed on properly sideways, which is a save a rider has not earned. The shipped default is **0.6** — past the knee, well short of that, and higher than the air's 0.5 because the two hands are not spending the same thing: the air's rotates a hull the rider can see it rotate, while this one takes a slide out of half a second on a deck and is invisible when it is not needed. Like the air's it is read and never written during a run and draws no randomness, so a run replays identically at any setting.

## The score (`tricks.ts`)

The clock is the race. The SCORE is the other game on the same water, and it pays for the parts of a run the clock has no opinion about: the time the hull spends off the water, the revolutions it turns nose-over-tail while it is up there (THE PUMP), the revolutions it turns about its own length (THE WHIP), and the flight itself as an element beside any of them. Every number is `TUNING.tricks`, and every one of them is an arcade dial — none is measured against anything, and what they are chosen against is the LADDER they make between one flight and the next.

**Air time pays by the second, at a rate that rises with the flight.** `airPointsPerSecond(t) = airRate · log2(1 + t / airKnee)` — 100 points a second one knee (1 s) into a flight, half again that at three seconds, about four and a half times it at twenty. A flight's whole purse is that rate integrated over it, which grows rather faster than the flight does:

| Flight | Rate at the end | Purse                                             |
| ------ | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| 0.5 s  | 58 pts/s        | 0 — under `flight.airCounts`, not air time at all |
| 1 s    | 100 pts/s       | 40                                                |
| 2 s    | 158 pts/s       | 172                                               |
| 5 s    | 258 pts/s       | 815                                               |
| 20 s   | 439 pts/s       | 6 324                                             |

(The purse is the rate integrated from the counting line, which is where the ticking starts — so the first half-second of every flight is free.) Twenty times the flight is a hundred and fifty-seven times the money, and the rate itself never runs away. That is the shape the whole thing is for: a rider clearing a two-metre chop is not doing what a rider going over the top of a storm sea is doing, and a score that paid them alike per second would say he was. Nothing ticks below `flight.airCounts` (0.5 s) — a hull skipping off a crest did not go anywhere, and in a head sea it does that a fifth of the steps.

**A revolution raises the multiplier, and the next one raises it more.** The rotation is the body pitch rate summed while aloft (`−wx · dt`, the same reading `flight_test` measures a backflip with) — the flip axis whatever attitude the hull is in, and a sum that comes to nothing in chop, so a hull being thrown about cannot accumulate a flip. Each whole turn is scored the instant it closes, in the air: the Nth revolution of a flight is worth `N · flipPoints` of base and `N` steps of multiplier, the multiplier starting at 1.

**A side spin climbs the same ladder, and climbs it separately.** The roll is the body ROLL rate summed while aloft (`−wz · dt`), the hull's own length as the axis, and each whole turn is worth `N · rollPoints` of base and `N` steps of multiplier exactly as a flip is. `rollPoints` is the same 300 as `flipPoints`, because the two are the same commitment asked of the same rider on two axes — the roll is the easier one to start and the harder one to hold together, since the hull has to come back the right way up for a landing the flip would have levelled it for. Naming them equal is also what makes them worth CHAINING: a rider who could earn more by flipping twice would never roll. The two counts are kept APART, so a flip with a roll in it is two FIRST revolutions and not one second one — which is what stops a corkscrew being priced as a double of either.

**And so does the air they were turned in, once something was turned in it.** A flight past `tricks.airElement` (0.5 s) is an element of the combo like any other and worth one step of multiplier — but it is only ever credited beside a trick, and only once per combo. Both halves are load-bearing. Credit it on its own and every jump on the course reads ×2, which is a multiplier that has stopped saying anything; credit it per flight and a rider climbs the ladder by hopping off crests. It adds NO base: the air is already paid by the second, and paying it twice would be the same seconds sold at two prices.

| Flight               | Base                  | Multiplier | Combo  |
| -------------------- | --------------------- | ---------- | ------ |
| 2 s, flat            | 172                   | ×1         | 172    |
| 2 s + backflip       | 172 + 300             | ×3         | 1 417  |
| 2 s + barrel roll    | 172 + 300             | ×3         | 1 417  |
| 2 s + both           | 172 + 300 + 300       | ×4         | 3 089  |
| 4 s + double flip    | 569 + 300 + 600       | ×5         | 7 347  |
| 4 s + flip + 2× roll | 569 + 300 + 300 + 600 | ×6         | 10 617 |

A double is ×4 of its own revolutions rather than ×3 on purpose: it is not two backflips, it is one much harder trick that happens to be measured in revolutions, and the ladder has to say so or nobody goes for the second one.

**Nothing is banked until the combo closes.** The base and the multiplier ride together while the hull is up and for `linkWindow` (1 s) after it comes down, so a landing straight into the next launch is ONE combo at one multiplier rather than two small ones. The window running out with the rider still on the craft banks `base × mult` into `tricks.score` and emits `combo`. Going over the bars does not: a `capsize`, a `dive` (the bow buried on the landing — the same mistake at the other end of the flight) or the rider putting himself back at a gate emits `bail` and the combo is gone. A `hit` is not a bail — a hull glancing off a skerry is still under its rider.

The engine only says what happened. `trick`, `combo` and `bail` are the events, `GameState.tricks` is the state a readout reads, and nothing here draws or draws randomness — a run replays to the same score.

**No word for any of it is in the engine.** `TrickKind` names the THING (`backflip`, `frontflip`, `roll`, `air`) and `TrickPart` carries how many revolutions of it and which flight of the combo it was won in; the WORDS are the app's, in the one table every line the player reads comes from (`pwa/src/game/strings.ts`, §39.1). That table is also where the vocabulary's one compound lives: a flight that turned exactly one flip and exactly one roll is a CORKSCREW, named once in place of both — which is what the flight index is carried for, since the same two elements taken off two waves in a row are a link and not a corkscrew. `STRINGS.comboLine` joins the elements the way an arcade skating game joins them, and the HUD flashes that line over the nose above the figure and the multiplier.

## Contacts (`collision.ts`)

- **Ground and ramps** are penalty contacts on the probes: a probe under the surface is pushed back along the surface's normal by a spring `contact.stiffness` = 90 000 N/m and a damper `damping` = 3200 N·s/m per probe (stiff enough that a hull riding a ramp sinks millimetres, damped near critical — and the reason the engine runs at 120 Hz: 60 cannot follow that spring), with Coulomb friction against the tangential slide, `groundFriction` = 0.45 on rock and sand, `rampFriction` = 0.08 on a wet deck. The ground's normal is the heightfield's gradient. A ramp is a plane hinged at the water at its rear edge (`(x, z)` is the HINGE — the one statement of the anchor is `rampSurface` in `mapgen/course.ts`; `rampDeckY` here is the same line), rising `angle` toward its front, with a submerged approach lip half its length behind the hinge so a hull slides onto it rather than hitting a step. A ramp is therefore a WEDGE rather than a plane: under the deck stand three walls — the two flanks, and the end wall under the lip — and the hinge end, where the deck meets the water, is the one side a hull may ride on from. A probe more than `rampWallBelow` = 0.3 m under the deck (measured normal to it) did not sink through the deck; it came in through whichever wall is the SHALLOWEST way back out of the wedge, and is pushed out through that one, horizontally, by its overlap, with the ground's friction and no cap (a wall has to stop a hull). Under that depth the probe is riding the deck, which is what lets a hull grazing the ramp near its hinge — where the deck stands centimetres up — climb aboard rather than be deflected. A deep probe far from every wall is a hull slammed onto the MIDDLE of the deck, and the deck pushes back, capped at `rampDeckCap` = 20 000 N a probe. Only a deck contact sets `onRamp`; a wall push does not. The end wall is what makes riding at a ramp from downrange a crash instead of a launch: the deck's normal points UP, so a hull meeting a deck two metres over its head through the lip's end face was thrown the height of the lip's penetration — tens of metres, tumbling.
- **Another hull** (`rivals.ts`, a race only) is the solids' contact with two hulls in it: each is three keel points with a half-beam round every one, the closest pair of points is pushed apart mass-weighted and given an impulse along the line between them with `RACE.bump.restitution` = 0.4 coming back and `tangentKeep` = 0.9 of the slide kept, and the offset from each centre of gravity turns some of the impulse into yaw on both — a shoulder taken off-centre swings a nose. Two hulls more than `bumpClearance` = 1.6 m apart in height are not touching (one is flying over the other). The player's contact is reported as `bump` when the closing speed is at least `bump.speed` = 1.2 m/s, at most every `bump.cooldown` = 0.5 s; a rival's own bumps stay on its own run.
- **Solids** (skerries, boulders, reefs) are vertical cylinders resolved as an impulse at three keel points (stern, middle, bow) against the hull's plan radius `hullRadius` = 0.9 of the half-beam: the hull is pushed out along the radial, the closing speed is reversed by `restitution` = 0.25, `tangentKeep` = 0.85 of the slide is kept, and the contact's offset from the centre of gravity turns the impulse into yaw. A reef whose top the keel clears is not a contact. A `hit` is reported when the closing speed or the craft's speed is at least `hitSpeed` = 1 m/s (a scrape along a rock at pace is a hit; a hull nudging one at rest is not), at most every `hitCooldown` = 0.35 s, carrying the closing speed.
- **The bounds** push softly back inside: past `boundsMargin` = 5 m from the edge, an acceleration of `boundsSpring` = 4 m/s² per metre of overshoot, clamped at ±40 m/s² — a slope, never a wall. They hold a rider in only where the level's rim is LAND, though: **the sea has no far side**. A rim standing in more than `boundsOpenDepth` = 15 m of water is the open ocean, and a rider who points at it rides straight out of the level and on into the storm — see [the open ocean](water.md#the-open-ocean-past-the-rim-enginegameoceants). Each axis's spring also asks that he still be within the box along the other one, so a rider a kilometre out is not reeled sideways by a land rim he is now abeam of, and the ground he would run onto past a rim is `bedAt`'s ocean floor rather than the last row of cells repeated forever.
- A `ground` event is reported when the keel meets the ground closing at over 0.4 m/s, at most every `groundCooldown` = 0.5 s.

`tests/collision_test.ts` holds a skerry pushing the hull out and reporting, a beach grounding it, a ramp carrying it up its deck, a cleared reef being no contact, and the edge being a slope.

## The capsize

A PWC does not self-right; the rider does. A hull on its back — its up pointing down — and not in the air for `capsize.after` = 1.5 s is over for good: a `capsize` event fires and the rider hauls it back over. For `capsize.righting` = 1 s the pitch and the roll are scaled back to zero over the countdown with the heading kept (the shortest way up), the angular rates are zeroed, the way is scrubbed off with a time constant of `capsize.slow` = 0.15 s (the vertical speed dropped outright), the hull is eased to its rest draft on the surface and the engine idles with the throttle closed; nothing else acts on the hull meanwhile — the rider is sitting on it with his weight thrown at the side it has to come back toward. The countdown is not a constant rate: the share of the angle still standing is smoothstep's complement (`standing` in `craft.ts`), so the hull hangs while he gets to work, comes over fastest as it passes its own beam, and settles the last few degrees instead of stopping dead — and each step takes the RATIO of that across the step, so the angles telescope to exactly zero on the last one. The app draws the whole second of it: the figure turns with the hull, upside down with it, rocking at the lean (`rider-pose.ts`'s `HAUL`), and the sea comes off both of them as it goes — a sheet off the flank being carried over the top, the water running off the man as the hull turns him out of the sea, the burst where he comes through it, and the slap of that flank coming down flat as the hull levels (`spray.ts`). This is the one branch of `stepCraft` that is a STATE rather than a force (`craft.righting` counts it down; `capsizedFor` counts toward it and resets to zero the moment the hull is upright or airborne), and it is deterministic: `tests/buoyancy_test.ts` holds an inverted hull afloat for the first 1.2 s with no event, the event firing exactly once, the engine idling through a held throttle while righting, the hull back within 0.1 m of its draft, and two capsizes righting the same way twice. The bot does not reset on a capsize; it waits.

## Gates and the reset (`course.ts`)

A WATER gate is a line between two buoys `width` apart (the rule book's `gate.width` = 12 m; the synthetic level's are 16 m), crossed by a move through it in the facing direction; an AIR gate is a ring of `width` diameter (`air.width` = 6 m generated; 7 m synthetic) whose centre stands `y` metres up, passed by a move through its disc. A gate is reached by going THROUGH it and by nothing else. Crossing the OWED gate's plane outside that opening is the miss itself: `missedGate` fires on that step, `course.missedPenalty` = 5 s is charged, and the run moves on while the checkpoint is still beside the rider. That missed checkpoint remains the HUD's informational return target — without changing the gate now owed — until the craft comes back inside its opening, so the warning, arrow and distance do not expire while the rider is still looking for it. `progress.activeMissedGate` is the whole of it, and the MARKS on the water read the same number: while it stands, that gate's buoys and ring are painted and lit in the warning's own red (`missedLamp`, `pwa/src/game/gates.ts`) rather than falling into the dull tone a gate already behind wears, so the water and the HUD cannot disagree about which checkpoint is still the rider's or how close to it they have to get. The amber lantern hands over to the gate now owed regardless, because that is still where the run is going — and that lantern is the DARK's alone: `markLamp` scales the whole lamp by the sky's own switch (`Preset.lamps`), so exactly one checkpoint burns at a time and none of them burns by day, when the course is read off the paint instead. A circuit's rounding buoys answer the same two questions in `pwa/src/game/buoys.ts`, off the charted flash character the generator drew (`buoyLightAt`): the can the run is riding at is lit — plus the one a rider is being sent back to — and only after dark. A CHECKPOINT IS ONLY VALID IN ITS TURN: the owed gate is the only one a move can take, so threading the gate after it — or the four after that — while this one is still owed counts for nothing at all, charges nothing, and leaves the run owing the gate it owed. The wide crossing above is the whole of the way forward: a rider who went past a checkpoint pays for it on its own plane and rides on, and a rider who is somehow beyond it without ever having crossed that plane rides back to it or takes the reset, which stands them behind it facing the right way. The finish is crossed like any other gate and cannot be paid past: there is no wide crossing of a finish line. `reset` stands the craft `resetBack` = 6 m behind the last gate it is DONE with — passed or paid for, which is the gate before the next one and not the last one it actually threaded — along that gate's own facing (or at the start), heading for the next, and `standCraft` — the one way anything puts a craft down — leaves it at rest at its rest draft, engine idling, nozzle centred, rider centred, with every cooldown and flag cleared (`hitCooldown`, `groundCooldown`, `dived`, `launchPending`, `capsizedFor`, `righting`; a craft stood here has hit nothing and landed nowhere, and a cooldown carried over would read as a hull still wedged). `resetCraft` then records `progress.lastResetAt` = the run clock, which is what the bot's idle timer counts from. `tests/course_test.ts` holds all of it.

## The integration

Velocity first, then position off the new velocity (semi-implicit Euler); the torques summed in the body frame with the gyroscopic term `ω × Iω` kept — negligible for a hull afloat, and the thing that makes a tumbling one tumble the way a real body does; the orientation quaternion integrated from the body rates. Two ceilings nothing honest reaches, `hull.maxSpin` = 25 rad/s and `hull.maxSpeed` = 80 m/s, are the wall between a contact that goes wrong and a NaN — `tests/simulation_test.ts` rides sixty seconds of chop, a gale, a capsize and a beach and holds every number finite. Then the readings: `heading`, `pitch`, `roll` off the quaternion (`lib/quat.ts` owns the sign flip: pitch nose-up positive, roll right-side-down positive, rates right-handed), `speed` = |v| (what the speedo reads), `wetted`, `submergedDepth`, `onRamp`, `onGround`, `landing` (seconds since the last landing).

## The catalog (`defs/craft.ts`)

Four answers to the same shore, not four points on one scale, and each answers to a real ARCHETYPE of personal watercraft — a rec-lite `runabout`, a supercharged `musclecraft`, a `tourer`, and a freestyle `stand-up`. The archetype is what decides where in the class's range each row sits, and it is stated on the row (`spec.archetype`) rather than left to the blurb.

**What a watercraft has that a car does not.** A car's sheet is torque, gears, redline, top speed. A watercraft has NO GEARBOX and no clutch — the impeller is bolted to the crank, so `impellerPitch` is the one fixed gear and the pump's own load is what the engine revs against. What it has instead, and what the archetypes differ on: the PUMP (`nozzleDiameter`, `impellerPitch`), FORCED INDUCTION (`boost`), the REVERSE BUCKET (`bucket` — the only brake there is), VARIABLE TRIM (`trimRange`), the SPONSONS (`sponsonBite`), the RIDE PLATE (`ridePlate`), the BOW'S ROCKER (`bowRise`), and THE RIDER (`riderAuthority`). Each is described where its model lives, above.

None of them is real: the numbers sit inside the published range for personal watercraft (dry mass 150–420 kg, 60–230 kW, 70–110 km/h, deadrise 16–24°) without being any one manufacturer's row, and `tests/craft_test.ts` holds the range. `topSpeed` and `accel0to50` are DERIVED EXPECTATIONS, not inputs: the physics is what makes the craft go that fast, and the test holds the physics to reproducing each within 10 % (top) and 20 % (0–50) on flat water — the measured columns below are that run, on the synthetic strip at density 1005 kg/m³.

## The input contract

```ts
type CraftInput = {
  steer: number; // −1..1, positive = CLOCKWISE seen from above (right in map view)
  throttle: number; // 0..1, analogue — the throttle lever
  reverse: number; // 0..1, analogue — the BRAKE AND REVERSE lever: the bucket
  lean: number; // −1..1, +1 = the rider leans BACK (nose up); in the air, the pitch
  //             control; afloat it also carries the nozzle's TRIM
  crouch: number; // 0..1, analogue — the TUCK: down behind the bars (SHIFT; keys only)
  reset: boolean; // edge: back to the last gate passed, facing the next, at rest
};
```

No handbrake and no gears, and the one brake is the BUCKET rather than a pedal: `reverse` drops the gate over the jet and opens the throttle enough to feed it, so the engine never runs backwards and the two levers stay separate the way they are on the bars. A craft with no bucket fitted ignores it entirely. The screen-side sign flip (the chase camera's view mirrors the map) lives in `pwa/src/game/input-model.ts` and nowhere else.

## What holds it

`tests/buoyancy_test.ts` (Archimedes, the draft, righting), `tests/craft_test.ts` (the sheet, the pump, the steering), `tests/flight_test.ts` (the arc, the landing, the dive's cost, the backflip, the quaternion algebra), `tests/tricks_test.ts` (the score: the air's rate, a flight's purse, the revolution ladder on both axes, the air as an element, the combo banked and bailed), `tests/tuck_test.ts` (what the tuck buys and what it costs), `tests/collision_test.ts`, `tests/course_test.ts`, `tests/place_test.ts`, `tests/simulation_test.ts` (nothing explodes; the bot finishes), `tests/determinism_test.ts`. **`make ride SCENARIO=`** (`scripts/ride-lab.mjs`) is the lab: the craft in profile every sixth of a second over the water it crossed, with speed, pitch, wetted share, rpm and air time beside each cell — required before and after any change to the hull, the planing lift, the slamming or the flight.

## What is NOT modelled

- **Ventilation and cavitation** of the pump — thrust never breaks away at high rpm or in aerated water; the intake is either fed or dry.
- **Trim tabs** — the nozzle trims and the bucket reverses (both above), but there is no separate transom tab.
- **Rider body dynamics** — the rider is a lagged point mass; no legs absorbing a slam, no falling off. He does now STAND (`CraftState.stand`, above) and TUCK, but each is a lagged scalar moving that one mass, not a body. His authority under the water (`submerged.ts`, above) is a torque per unit input like the air's, not a body bracing against a hull. The capsize is the one other thing the rider "does", and it is a timer (`wipeout` — the rider thrown, the swim back — is a reserved skill). The pull in the air is one impulse at a fixed size, not a body moving.
- **Windage as a shape** — the air drag is two points (`windageY`/`windageZ` fore-and-aft, `windageSideZ` on the beam) with two areas, and no more: there is no lift off the deck, no yaw-dependent centre of pressure travel, and a rider crouched or standing changes only `tuck.dragCut` and where the push acts.
- **Which chine is wet** as the carve's measure — the carve reads the ROLL ANGLE past a dead band; `liftX`, where the wet bottom's centre actually sits, is computed and read by nothing.
- **Damage** — `engine/game/damage.ts` is a placeholder; a hit costs speed and heading, never the machinery.
- **Added mass** beyond the slam and the lumped rotational damping — a submerged hull displaces some three times its own mass of water and has to rotate it, which would slow every attitude it reaches under the surface; benched, adding it also made a craft tossed onto its back too slow to settle there to capsize at all, so it waits for a pass that owns the capsize too; **hull flex**; **spray** as a force; **wave-making** beyond the form drag.
- **Porpoising** — clamped away by `clMax` rather than reproduced.
- **The duct's own losses** — the intake's RAM DRAG is modelled (`intakeDrag`, above), but as the momentum term alone: the duct's friction and bend losses, and the lip separation of an intake being fed faster than the pump is swallowing, are not. Going ASTERN the hull is not a hull but a flat transom, and that much IS modelled: `hull.asternCd` = 1.1 against the fine end's 0.14, with no planing fade over it, which is why a craft backs up at walking pace however hard the bucket pushes.
- **Fuel and its mass**, water in the hull, temperature effects on the water or the engine.
- **Every trick but the two that are reachable** — the score (above) pays for air time and for revolutions turned nose-over-tail, because a hull that can be thrown into a backflip is what the physics offers. A barrel roll, a hull held on its tail, a trick taken off a buoy and a landing scored for how it was taken are all the same machinery with another term in it, and none of them is written.
