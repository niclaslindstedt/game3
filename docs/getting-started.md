# Getting started

## Playing

Open [game3.niclaslindstedt.se](https://game3.niclaslindstedt.se/). The house's name comes up while the first shore is built, then the title and an invitation; press anything and you are on the front door — **START** and **OPTIONS**, over a sea that is already running, ridden by the bot. START opens the start card, which asks where the run is and what day it is in: which **shore** (a seed, with the coast it makes charted underneath it — the whole course, its shallows, its skerries and the line through its gates), what **season** (spring, summer, autumn or winter — the sun's arc, which is how long the day is and how dark the night gets), what **time** of day to start at (sunrise, day, sunset or night, worked out from this coast's own daylight in that season — and the clock runs on from there at an hour a minute, so a sunset start rides into the twilight, the dark, the moon on the water, and the craft's lamp comes on, while a night start begins out there under the stars and rides out of it into the dawn; night is the one answer no seed is ever dealt, so it is never the marked one), what **wind** (calm, brisk or storm — the wind is what builds the sea), and what **weather** (clear, high cloud, cloudy, rainy or squall). Every row is the same knob — the name, the value between two arrows, and the pips under it saying where on its ladder that value stands. Nothing on it arrives with an opinion: the answer each row already has from the seed is the one **marked with a dot**, its pip ringed on the ladder so you can see which way back is, and leaving it there rides the level exactly as it was generated. Step back onto the marked value to come back to it after choosing something else. WEATHER's mark follows the WIND row while a wind is chosen, because the sky that belongs over that wind is what you get by not touching it — so the water and the ceiling only come apart when you deliberately pull them apart. **NEXT**, in the card's top-right corner, then opens the second card: the hull turning on the water at the draft it really floats at, with its top speed, its sprint and how it compares with the rest of the roster beside it, because four hulls are four shapes and a row of names cannot show one. **RIDE** there stands the run up behind a loading card and hands it to you: a generated stretch of taiga shore, the sea beside it, a course of buoy gates laid along the coast, and your craft behind the first gate. The run starts when you open the throttle, and the clock with it. **Escape** leaves a run for the front door again.

**Options.** The camera a run opens on — the ladder is **BOW** out on the foredeck, **NOSE** over the handlebars, **CLOSE** over the transom, **CHASE** (the one the game is tuned to be read at), **FAR** stood back, and **HELI** flown high and behind — what the picture costs, and whether the HUD is drawn — remembered between visits, along with everything the start card and the craft card set. **SOUND** is one fader over every sound effect — the engine and the pump, the spray and the wind, the sea and every splash — reading OFF at the bottom of its travel; a MUSIC fader arrives with the music. **VIBRATION**, under the camera, is the sea through the bars: on a phone the bottom slapping the chop, every landing and every rock come back through the motor, sized by how hard the hull actually took them. It is only offered on a device that can be felt — a desktop browser answers the vibration API and does nothing with it, so there is no row there. There are no key bindings because the keys below are fixed; that row arrives the day the thing behind it does.

The picture is six rows, and they are pressed over the live sea behind the card so every one of them shows on the water while you are still looking at it. **WATER** is the one to reach for first, in either direction, and it is the whole sea in one word. Half of it is the surface: how fine the wave grid is under you and how far the shader's ripples survive before the water flattens into facets (how far out that grid REACHES is DISTANCE's, below) — the only part of the page whose bill is the processor rather than the graphics card, and HIGH costs about half again as much of it as the design point. The other half is everything drawn on that surface: the spray off the hull, the wake behind it — at LOW the road and the fan are foam on a sea that does not bend for them, which is the arcade generation's own wake and half the price of the relief — what a landing, a dive or a capsize does to the sea (at LOW a plume and a patch of foam, at MEDIUM the crater the hull knocks in the water and the boil round one on its back, at HIGH the ring wave rolling out of it and the whole wall of water a dive throws over the rider), and whether the shore, the wood on it and the craft stand mirrored in the water — a second, small drawing of the coast from under the surface, which is the dearest thing on the row. The two halves move together because a coarse sea still carrying every droplet and a sharp mirror is neither picture: it costs what you were trying not to spend, and looks worse than the stop you came from. **RESOLUTION** is what share of your screen's own pixels the frame is drawn at — half of them at each stop down — and it is the biggest lever there is on a phone that is struggling. **DETAIL** is everything around the water, as one choice: the sea life under the hull, the tree line behind the shore, how much sky there is — how many sheets of cloud are drawn and how much structure each has, which is paid twice because the sea reflects them — and how much rain is in the air and whether a downpour pocks the water with rings. Nothing on it touches what the sea itself is drawn like, so turning it up on a machine you have already asked for a cheap sea cannot spend that back: DETAIL at HIGH over WATER at LOW is a thick shore under a heavy sky over the same cheap water. **DISTANCE** is how much WORLD there is: the sea drawn as a sea — with its own chop on it rather than the long swell alone — out to a reach that doubles at every stop, and then the shore out to a radius, the wood on it to a shorter one, the grass and the heather along it to shorter ones still — a plant is drawn while it is big enough on the screen to tell from the shore behind it, and a 40 cm mat stops being that long before a spruce does — and the fog brought in to meet all of them, so what LOW buys is not a shore that stops but a hazier day, which is the answer the arcade generation this game is measured against reached for too. The coast half is the cheapest frames on the page, because everything it takes away was already behind the weather or too small to make out; the sea half is the one part of the row that costs something, and it is what stops the water's chop ending in open view with a flat plate beyond it — which from the standoff and helicopter seats is the first thing the eye finds. **SEE-THROUGH** is the sea as a window: the bed coming up under the shallows, a rock's foot, a school crossing under the hull. Off, the sea is solid and cheaper, and a little brighter for having nothing dark behind it. **FRAME RATE** is not a picture cost but a schedule: the most frames a second the game draws — the screen's own rate at MAX, or held to 60 or 30, which on a machine that cannot keep its screen's rate is the same work drawn evenly rather than in bursts. **FPS**, in the HUD group beside them, puts the frame rate under the minimap, which is how any of the six is judged.

**The developer page.** Hold **START** for seven seconds and it appears under OPTIONS. Behind it, in the options page's own two columns, are the same seed the start card picks, a wind and a sea in exact figures to override the level's own, the staged moments, and a COPY REPRO LINK that writes the lot back out as a URL — which is the point of it: a frame you found is a frame you can hand to somebody else.

**What a run is.** The course is a line of gates, in order. A **water gate** is two buoys; cross the line between them, the right way round, and it counts. An **air gate** is a ring hanging over the water with a floating **ramp** moored before it: hit the ramp with pace, lean back, and fly through the ring. Skip a gate by passing the one after it and the missed one is still counted as reached, with a penalty on the clock. The last gate is the finish. **R** puts you back at the last gate you passed, facing the next one; **B** restarts the whole run on the same shore.

**Choosing a level.** Every level is generated from a seed, so a URL is a level: `?seed=38` opens seed 38, and `?craft=marlin` picks the craft (`skiff`, `marlin`, `otter`, `dart`); `?camera=heli` opens on a rung of the camera ladder; add `?start=1` to skip the cards and ride it. The seed is in the HUD corner, beside the build label — send both with a bug report and whoever reads it stands on the same water. The whole set of parameters is in [configuration.md](configuration.md).

**The craft.** Four ship, and what separates them is real physics off a data card rather than a badge: the **skiff** (a light runabout — quick, nimble, skittish in chop), the **marlin** (heavy performance — the fastest, and it needs room), the **otter** (stable touring — the heaviest, the softest over waves, slow to turn) and the **dart** (a stand-up — the lightest, the most agile, the least stable). The rider on the saddle is drawn from the engine's own readings: the lean you input moves a point mass in the physics, and the figure slides and leans where that mass went, tucks into the wind as the pump opens, hangs into a turn, and takes a landing in his knees and back.

### Controls

**Desktop (keyboard).**

| Key          | Action                                                                                                |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| W            | Throttle — analogue in spirit: it ramps up while held                                                 |
| ← → / A D    | Steer — ramped, so a tap is a nudge and a hold is full lock                                           |
| ↓            | Lean back — nose up; in the air, pitch the nose up                                                    |
| ↑ / Q        | Lean forward — nose down; in the air, pitch the nose down                                             |
| Shift (hold) | **Tuck** — get down behind the bars: less air resistance, less of everything you steer with your body |
| S / Space    | Brake and reverse — the bucket over the jet                                                           |
| R            | Back to the last gate passed (also the HUD button)                                                    |
| B            | Restart the run from the beginning                                                                    |
| Enter        | Take a screenshot — filed, and copied to the clipboard                                                |
| C            | Camera: the next rung of the ladder (also the HUD button)                                             |
| Escape       | Pause: hold the run and put the pause card up                                                         |

The left hand gets the driving set — W on the throttle, S on the brake, A and D steering — and the arrow cluster is the handlebar: ← → steer it, ↑ ↓ lean on it, the same way the thumb's bar on a phone does — pull toward you for nose up, push away for nose down.

**None of it is fixed.** OPTIONS ▸ KEYBOARD lists every action on the craft and every press around a run; press a row, then press the key you want on it, and that one key becomes the whole binding. Escape backs out of a row without changing anything — which is why it is the one key the page will not bind, since it is the way out of every surface in the game. A key may end up on two actions at once if you want it there; the row says ALSO ON when it is, and both things happen on the press. RESET KEYS puts the shipped layout back without touching anything else you have chosen. The page is only offered on a machine with keys — a phone is shown the rest of OPTIONS and not this.

**The tuck.** Hold SHIFT and the rider gets down behind the bars. It takes about a fifth off the hole the craft makes in the air, which on flat calm water is worth about a km/h at the top — a watercraft is stopped by the water, not by the air, so do not expect a new machine. Punching into a blow is where it lands: drag goes as the closing speed squared, so into 14 m/s of head wind the skiff takes the last stretch to 90 km/h in half the time tucked. It costs you the turn, because on a watercraft you steer with your body as much as with the bars: tucked, you cannot slide back down the seat, hang off the side, sweep the bars their full throw, or throw your weight about in the air. The stand-up pays most, since that is nearly all it steers with. Getting down and back up takes a moment — it is a body, not a switch — so a tuck grabbed on the way into a buoy is still costing you at the apex. It is a keyboard control and has no touch equivalent: both thumbs are already busy.

On a menu card the arrow keys (or WASD) walk the rows, Enter or Space presses one, and Escape goes back a page.

**Screenshots.** **Enter** takes a picture of the frame you are looking at — the sea, the craft and the sky the renderer drew, with the HUD over it as it stood when you pressed, and the game's mark in the corner. **On a phone you take it the way you take every other picture on that phone** — the hardware's own shutter; there is no button for it over the water, because the top strip is the one part of the screen that has to stay clear of the sea. In the **App Store / Play Store app** the game is told when you do that and files its own copy alongside, so a picture taken with the side buttons lands in the gallery below as well as in the phone's camera roll (iOS anywhere, Android 14 and up — it costs no permission either way, and in a mobile browser the phone's screenshot stays the phone's alone). The picture lands a frame or three later and the news column says so; the run never stops for it. **Every picture also goes straight onto the clipboard**, so it can be pasted into a chat or a document without going through the gallery — the receipt reads PICTURE SAVED · COPIED once the clipboard has it, and PICTURE SAVED alone in a browser with no image writer (Firefox). Every picture is kept in the browser on the machine it was taken on — the last forty, oldest falling off — and **GALLERY** on the front door is where they are looked at, flipped through, and **shared**, **copied** or **saved** to a file. Nothing is ever uploaded anywhere.

**Pausing.** Press Escape, or press the **minimap** — on a phone the map is the way in, since there is no Escape key to reach for. The run stops exactly where it stands: the wave holds, the spray hangs, the clock waits. The card carries **RESUME** (also what Escape and a press on the backdrop do, because a card opened by a thumb aiming for the map has to cost one press to leave), then a short strip of the settings a rider actually stops mid-run for — the **sound** too loud for the room, the **camera** you cannot see out of, the **HUD** in the way of the water, and the **frame rate** — and then **MAIN MENU**, which hands the same craft back to the bot and comes back to the front door over the shore you were just on. The full OPTIONS page is not offered here: a picture row is judged against a sea that is moving, and the one thing this card does is stop it.

There is no handbrake and no gear: a jet ski has a throttle, a nozzle, and — on most of them — a bucket. Which means the one thing to learn early: **no throttle, almost no steering**. The nozzle turns the thrust; with no thrust there is nothing to turn. Off the throttle the hull keeps going where it was pointed, with only the keel's small say in the matter — letting go is measurably the worst thing you can do to a corner, and it is the real machine's real hazard rather than a quirk of ours. To turn, stay on the gas. To turn when you are carrying too much speed for the buoy, brake INTO it and get back on the gas — the gate scrubs the speed and keeps the nozzle flowing, which is the only hand that does both, and a braked corner comes round in about half the water a throttled one needs.

**The brake is the bucket** — and it is how you make a corner at speed. S or Space (or dragging the right thumb up past the shut mark) swings a clamshell down over the jet: the flow goes forward and under instead of back, and the gate itself becomes a plate hanging in the water, which is most of the braking once you are moving. It stops the craft hard — about half a g, twice what letting go of the throttle gives you — puts the bow down while it does, and held past the stop it backs you up at a walking pace — and because the nozzle sits upstream of the gate, the steering works BACKWARDS in reverse. **Hold it with the bars turned and it is the tightest line the craft has**: the buried bow puts the forefoot and the front half of the keel into water a planing hull keeps them clear of, and the craft swings through a right angle in about half the water — and less time — than the same corner taken flat out. That is what the button is for once you already know how to stop. It takes a third of a second or so to swing, so it is a thing to reach for before the buoy rather than at it. You can see it working: the water either side of the hull erupts white and the craft sits in a pool of foam that reaches past the bow, and the rev bar's caption reads BRAKE — REVERSE once you are backing up — in the lever's own alarm colour. **The stand-up has no bucket at all** — no brake and no reverse — which is part of what makes it the hardest of the four to place on a tight line.

**Phone (touch).** The LOWER-LEFT of the screen is the handlebar: touch anywhere and move your thumb — sideways travel steers, vertical travel leans (down toward you is back, nose up). The LOWER-RIGHT is the LEVER, and the touch itself is WIDE OPEN — a finger landing anywhere in that half is already asking for everything the pump has, which is both what you want almost all the time and what a watercraft's own sprung finger lever does: you hold it on and ease it out. The throw runs UP the glass from where you touched, and it is one throw with two halves — about ninety pixels closes the throttle, and about sixty past that pulls the brake and reverse, drawn in the alarm colour so a thumb never has to ask which half it is in. Both are analogue, only one can be open at a time, held for as long as the finger is down, and let go the moment it lifts. Because the anchor is full throttle rather than nothing, a grip low on the glass costs you nothing: there is no travel to spend off the bottom of the phone. Both overlays are drawn under the thumb that owns them. There is no tuck on touch — both thumbs are already on the bar and the lever, and a third zone would have to be reached by letting go of one of them. Both stop at the lower three fifths of the screen, so the readouts along the top — and the RESET and CAMERA buttons among them — keep their own presses instead of losing them to the throttle. The one press that does stand inside a zone is the new-build mark in the bottom-right corner, which takes its own small patch of the lever's glass on the rare day there is a new build to offer. Works in portrait and landscape.

### How a run reads

The HUD is the game's own type over the water: **speed** big in km/h, the **rpm bar** under it (no gear to show), the **run clock** and **gate n / N** top left with the **last split** beside them and a **wind vane** under them — the direction it blows from and the metres per second — and, once a flight has lasted long enough to be one, an **air time** counter across the top centre of the frame — over the nose, where you are already looking to aim the landing — that runs until you touch down. Half a second is the line: a hop off a crest is not air time, so the clock stays out of the frame through the chop. It **fades** in rather than arriving, because a number that popped into the middle of the frame at the moment the hull left the water would be a flinch where you wanted a reading, and it **grows as the flight goes on**, so the shape of the thing over your nose already tells you whether this one is big before you have read a digit. Take the run's **longest flight** and the clock sticks where it is for a couple of seconds after you land instead of going out with the water — stopped on the number you flew, pulsing, with **RECORD** under it and a chime over the splash. The top-right corner is the **minimap**: a square of sea around you, drawn as the coast actually is — the water in three depths under a running swell, so you can see the bottom coming up before you are on it; the shore in the green of the wood it carries, with the bare stone of a headland above the tree line; a pale surf line between the two; and the rocks, the reefs, the racing line and the gates over it, the one you owe picked out, with a chevron on the frame pointing at it when it is off the window. **The window breathes with the speedo** — it closes right in at an idle, where the map is a plan of the water under the hull, and opens to most of half a kilometre on the plane, where it is the sea about to arrive — so what it holds is a roughly constant amount of warning rather than a constant piece of sea, and the coast pulling back as you wind up is something the eye reads as speed. The rule at its foot says what the picture is worth. The frame is the progress gauge, the figure opposite the rule is how far the next gate is, and pressing it holds the run. Hung under the map are the two presses a rider makes with the hull still moving: **RESET** back to the last gate, and **CAMERA** to the next view. The build label sits in the corner. Under all of it, when OPTIONS ▸ FPS asks for it, the frame rate — and under that, for anyone who has found the developer page, what the frame cost: the water's slice of the processor, the draw calls and the triangles. **The whole of it dims when the craft's lamp comes on.** The chrome was mixed to hold over a bright sea at noon, and over a coast lit by a headlamp and a moon the same white is glare in front of the dark you are trying to see into — so as the sun goes down the ink comes off pure white, the navy behind every figure goes to nearly black, and the chart's shore goes to slate. It rides the lamp rather than the clock, so it comes on with the beam and comes on the way the beam does, over the couple of minutes the sun takes to cross the horizon. What does not dim is what you would want woken by: the redline, the brake, a missed gate, your own mark on the chart, and the buoy orange of the gate you owe, which has to go on agreeing with the buoys out on the water.

**The water is the game.** The sea is a sum of real waves, built from the level's wind by a fetch-limited spectrum: even in the lee of the shore it runs half a metre, and the further out you ride the more the wind has had room to work on it, so the swell grows with distance from the coast — at a hull's pace a crest every second or so: the climb, the launch, the drop into the next face. It shoals as it comes in — steepening over the shallows, and breaking where it gets too tall for the depth under it. The hull floats on that surface by displacement, twelve probes reading the water under them; it lifts onto the plane as speed rises and the drag drops away through the hump; and it feels the orbital motion of the wave itself, so a crest gives it a shove and a trough takes one back. A hard landing decelerates hard; a nose-down landing DIVES — the bow buries, the drag spikes and the nose is dragged further down — which is what the lean is for.

**The air.** A ramp is a plane the hull rides up; leave it at speed and the craft is ballistic, with the wind leaning on it and the lean input pitching it. Level the hull before the water arrives and it lands clean; land nose-down and it dives.

**The backflip**, and how it is actually ridden: hold the lean back going up the face of a wave or up a ramp's deck, and then **TAP** it off the lip — every tap is another haul on the bars, and the hull comes round faster for each one. A hold alone is one haul and will only flip the lightest hull; the taps are what bring a **backflip** round off an ordinary ramp, in a tap or two on the stand-up and in six or seven on the tourer, because how much a haul is worth is the rider's own authority against the hull's pitch inertia. Stop tapping and it stops turning faster, which is how you aim the landing — and nothing is banked until the hull is back on the water under you.

### Installing on your phone

The game is a PWA. iOS Safari: Share → **Add to Home Screen**. Android Chrome: menu → **Install app** (or accept the install prompt). The installed game launches fullscreen, works offline once loaded, and shows an in-app prompt when a new build ships.

## Developing

```sh
git clone https://github.com/niclaslindstedt/game3
cd game3
npm install
npm run dev
```

Then read [architecture.md](architecture.md) for the layout and [CONTRIBUTING.md](../CONTRIBUTING.md) for the workflow. The tools you'll live in while tuning: `make sim` (does the change help or hurt, measured), `make waves` (what the sea is), `make ride` (what the hull does on it), `make level` (what the generator builds), `make screenshots` (what it looks like).
