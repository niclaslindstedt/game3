# Getting started

## Playing

Open [game3.niclaslindstedt.se](https://game3.niclaslindstedt.se/). The house's name comes up while the first shore is built, then the title and an invitation; press anything and you are on the front door — a block of tiles over a sea that is already running, ridden by the bot, each tile a mark and a name. **The colour says how close the press is to water**, which is the whole of what there is to learn about the card: the one ORANGE tile is the way in, the SEA BLUE ones are the other ways onto water, and the low strip along the foot is everything that is not riding. The orange one is **CAMPAIGN** — the game with something to ride for, below, twice the size of the rest and billed with how many of the pinned shores you have cleared so far — and the four beside it are the four ways onto any water at all: **RACE** (eleven others on the grid, off on the same GO, and the clock decides), **TRICKS** (the course taken off the shore, the ramps left standing and a line of them laid down it, a clock that runs down and a score that runs up), **TIME TRIAL** (the course against the clock with nobody else on it), and **FREE RIDE** (the same water with nothing asked of you — no buzzer, nobody else out there — and the weather set by hand). Along the foot, quietly, **GALLERY** and **OPTIONS** — and **DEVELOPER** once it has been found. Turn a phone on its side and the door lays itself out the other way round — the card widens, the campaign stands beside the four rather than above them — because sideways is how the game is actually held and there is no height to spend there. **RACE**, **TRICKS** and **TIME TRIAL** open the **level card**, titled with the game it is setting up, and it asks in two steps. First **which coast**: the campaign's own shores, a row each the width of the card with a render of that coast behind it — the game's own picture of the place, taken from the helicopter seat on the first level that shore opens on, panning slowly across the row — and, on one the campaign has not opened yet, a padlock and what opens it. Press a row and the second step is that shore's levels, the ones this game can be ridden on — a race shore takes RACE and TIME TRIAL, a tricks shore takes TRICKS, because the trick field is laid when the shore is built — with the way back at the head, so you step out the way you came in. Each box says what the run would be, the level's name, the day it pins and the best you have ridden it in, and behind the words is the **layout** of that level's racing line: the whole course drawn as a shape, so a shore's nine boxes read as nine different rides before a name on any of them has been read, and a lapped one reads as the loop it is. A shore opens here the moment the CAMPAIGN opens it, all of it at once, so the warm coast is there from the first visit and the cold one and the polar one arrive with the campaign; the level card is a place to ride the shores you have been given rather than a second ladder to climb. Riding the pinned shores rather than a number of your own is what makes a time a time: the same water, the same day, for everybody. A tricks run also carries its **LENGTH** row here — two, four or six minutes — since outside the campaign that is a real choice. **FREE RIDE** is the one tile that opens the **start card** instead, and it asks the shore and the day outright: which **coast** (the biome the shore is built on — the taiga, a cold skerry coast of granite and pine on grey-green water, the mangrove, a warm flat coast of white sand and palms on turquoise water, or the arctic, a polar coast where the shore is a wall of glacier ice, the river a crack in it, the water black and at the freezing point, and the winter sea a sheet of ice an icebreaker has cut the course through — each with its own sky, its own cover, its own birds and its own sea life), which **shore** (a seed, with the coast it makes charted underneath it — the whole course, its shallows, its skerries and the line through its gates), then what **season** (spring, summer, autumn or winter — the sun's arc, which is how long the day is and how dark the night gets), what **time** of day to start at (sunrise, day, sunset or night, worked out from this coast's own daylight in that season — and the clock runs on from there at an hour a minute, so a sunset start rides into the twilight, the dark, the moon on the water, and the craft's lamp comes on, while a night start begins out there under the stars and rides out of it into the dawn; night is the one answer no seed is ever dealt, so it is never the marked one), what **wind** (a fader, from a flat calm to forty metres a second — the wind is what builds the chop beside you, and it always blows straight in off the open water, square on to the average line of the shore, so the sea you ask for is the sea you get), what **waves** (a fader too, a metre to twenty — the GROUNDSWELL that has piled up out past the coast; it is somebody else's weather from days ago, so it owes the wind row nothing, and a flat blue morning can have ten metres rolling under it — and it is a baseline rather than a ceiling: ride out to sea and the ocean still builds on top of it), and what **weather** (clear, haze, high cloud, cloudy, rainy or squall — the skies that coast offers; the haze is the warm coast's own). Every worded row is the same knob — the name, the value between two arrows, and the pips under it saying where on its ladder that value stands; the two that hold a figure are faders instead, since nothing on a free ride is being compared with anything. Nothing on the free ride's card arrives with an opinion (the coast excepted, which is a choice no seed makes): the answer each row already has from the seed is the one **marked with a dot**, its pip ringed on the ladder so you can see which way back is, and leaving it there rides the level exactly as it was generated. Step back onto the marked value to come back to it after choosing something else. WAVES is the one row nothing above it moves — no wind implies a swell, which is the whole reason it is a row of its own — while WEATHER's mark follows the WIND row while a wind is chosen, because the sky that belongs over that wind is what you get by not touching it — so the water and the ceiling only come apart when you deliberately pull them apart. **NEXT** on the start card, and **RIDE** or a box on the level card, then open the second card: the hull turning on the water at the draft it really floats at, with its top speed, its sprint and how it compares with the rest of the roster beside it, because four hulls are four shapes and a row of names cannot show one. On a FREE RIDE there is a **CLASS** row under it — novice, stock, limited or open — which is a different race and not only a faster ski, since it derives a quicker hull AND paces the course for it. It is that card's alone and the other ways in do not draw it at all: they are all ridden stock, because a time or a score compared across classes is not a comparison, and the row comes back to the measured modes the day there is somebody to measure a rider against. **RIDE** there stands the run up behind a loading card and hands it to you: a generated stretch of shore on the coast you chose, the sea beside it, a course of buoy gates laid along the coast, and your craft behind the first gate. Not every number is a coast: the generator refuses a seed it cannot lay a clean shore and a clean course on — roughly one in a hundred — rather than fudging one, and the loading card says **NO COAST AT THIS SEED** and hands you back to the card that chose it — the level card, or the start card's seed row — to step to the next one. Three lights hold you there — the sea is already running under the hull and the engine is already idling, but nothing is steered and no clock runs until they go out. The run starts on GO, and the clock with it. **Escape** leaves a run for the front door again.

**Options.** The camera a run opens on — the ladder is **BOW** out on the foredeck, **NOSE** over the handlebars, **CLOSE** over the transom, **CHASE** (the one the game is tuned to be read at), **FAR** stood back, and **HELI** flown high and behind — what the picture costs, and whether the HUD is drawn — remembered between visits, along with everything the start card and the craft card set. **SOUND** is one fader over every sound effect — the engine and the pump, the spray and the wind, the sea and every splash — reading OFF at the bottom of its travel; a MUSIC fader arrives with the music. **VIBRATION**, under the camera, is the sea through the bars: on a phone the bottom slapping the chop, every landing and every rock come back through the motor, sized by how hard the hull actually took them. It is only offered on a device that can be felt — a desktop browser answers the vibration API and does nothing with it, so there is no row there. There are no key bindings because the keys below are fixed; that row arrives the day the thing behind it does.

The picture is seven rows, and they are pressed over the live sea behind the card so every one of them shows on the water while you are still looking at it. **WATER** is the one to reach for first, in either direction, and it is the whole sea in one word. Half of it is the surface: how fine the wave grid is under you and how far the shader's ripples survive before the water flattens into facets (how far out that grid REACHES is DISTANCE's, below) — the only part of the page whose bill is the processor rather than the graphics card, and HIGH costs about half again as much of it as the design point. The other half is everything drawn on that surface: the spray off the hull, the wake behind it — at LOW the road and the fan are foam on a sea that does not bend for them, which is the arcade generation's own wake and half the price of the relief — and what a landing, a dive or a capsize does to the sea (at LOW a plume and a patch of foam, at MEDIUM the crater the hull knocks in the water and the boil round one on its back, at HIGH the ring wave rolling out of it and the whole wall of water a dive throws over the rider). The two halves move together because a coarse sea still carrying every droplet is neither picture: it costs what you were trying not to spend, and looks worse than the stop you came from. **RESOLUTION** is what share of your screen's own pixels the frame is drawn at — half of them at each stop down — and it is the biggest lever there is on a phone that is struggling. **DETAIL** is everything around the water, as one choice: the sea life under the hull, the tree line behind the shore, how much sky there is — how many sheets of cloud are drawn and how much structure each has, which is paid twice because the sea reflects them — and how much rain is in the air and whether a downpour pocks the water with rings. Nothing on it touches what the sea itself is drawn like, so turning it up on a machine you have already asked for a cheap sea cannot spend that back: DETAIL at HIGH over WATER at LOW is a thick shore under a heavy sky over the same cheap water. **DISTANCE** is how much WORLD there is: the sea drawn as a sea — with its own chop on it rather than the long swell alone — out to a reach that doubles at every stop, and then the shore out to a radius, the wood on it to a shorter one, the grass and the heather along it to shorter ones still — a plant is drawn while it is big enough on the screen to tell from the shore behind it, and a 40 cm mat stops being that long before a spruce does — and the fog brought in to meet all of them, so what LOW buys is not a shore that stops but a hazier day, which is the answer the arcade generation this game is measured against reached for too. The coast half is the cheapest frames on the page, because everything it takes away was already behind the weather or too small to make out; the sea half is the one part of the row that costs something, and it is what stops the water's chop ending in open view with a flat plate beyond it — which from the standoff and helicopter seats is the first thing the eye finds. **SEE-THROUGH** is the sea as a window: the bed coming up under the shallows, a rock's foot, a school crossing under the hull. Off, the sea is solid and cheaper, and a little brighter for having nothing dark behind it. **REFLECTIONS** is the other half of that trade and the dearest single thing in the frame: the shore, the wood on it, the rocks and the craft drawn a SECOND time from under the surface so they stand in the water. On a coast that second drawing is about half of everything the frame submits, so the row is the first place to look on an older machine. OFF is the sea reflecting the sky alone — honest on open water, and a visibly flatter, paler picture in front of a shore — and it closes SEE-THROUGH with it, because a machine that cannot afford the one cannot usually afford the other; turning the mirror back up leaves the window where you put it. GLOW is the rung between: the coast is still drawn into the water, but into a picture a fifth of the size, read far more blurred, and redrawn every other frame — what comes back is the land's light and mass in the sea rather than its trees, for about a third of what the mirror costs. SOFT is the mirror the game is tuned on and SHARP keeps a tree line's trunks at the waterline a little longer. **FRAME RATE** is not a picture cost but a schedule: the most frames a second the game draws — the screen's own rate at MAX, or held to 60 or 30, which on a machine that cannot keep its screen's rate is the same work drawn evenly rather than in bursts. **FPS**, in the HUD group beside them, puts the frame rate under the minimap, which is how any of the seven is judged.

**The developer page.** Hold **RACE** for seven seconds and it appears under OPTIONS. Behind it are the instruments over a run — FRAME COST in the HUD's corner — a COPY REPRO LINK that writes the run back out as a URL, and the two pages under it: BENCHMARK's history, and **UNLOCKS**. The shore, the staged moment and the exact wind and sea a run is stood up in are still there, as the `?seed=`, `?scene=`, `?wind=` and `?hs=` parameters the repro link carries (`docs/configuration.md`); the seed and the day have rows a player turns on the start card, and one dial with two places to turn it is two answers to one question the first time they disagree.

**UNLOCKS** is the campaign as something to set rather than earn. Each shore has an UNLOCK and a LOCK, and both work on the ladder as a whole rather than on one rung: UNLOCK wins that shore and every shore before it, LOCK puts it and every shore after it back to never ridden — the campaign is a prefix, and it has no rung standing open behind one nobody has ridden. UNLOCK EVERYTHING and LOCK EVERYTHING take the lot in one press. What a grant writes is a WIN — you top of each level's board, the podium filled in behind you, the gold on a tricks rung — and what it does not write is a figure: nobody rode the level, so its box quotes no time and no score until somebody does, and the first real run down it sets one. Neither press touches the **record book**: that is a different store, and a lock puts the ladder back without costing a line of what was ridden for it. Opening a shore here opens it everywhere the campaign opens it, RACE, TRICKS and TIME TRIAL included.

Under those rows is the one press on that page that is not a setting: **BENCHMARK**. It takes the canvas for thirty seconds and times a fixed piece of riding — a twelve-hull race off one green, on a pinned shore under a pinned sky with four metres of swell running under it, drawn as fast as your machine will draw it. What comes back is an **INDEX**, where 100 is drawing the race in the time it takes to ride: 200 is twice that, 50 is half, and one machine's score divides into another's to say how many times faster it is. The card is a graph rather than a counter, because the number only means anything once it has settled — a gold line walking onto the score as the run goes, and a blue one under it showing the rate of each reading's own frame, so a machine that gets slower halfway leaves a kink where a single figure at the end would have hidden it. The one thing the run deliberately does NOT pin is OPTIONS ▸ VIDEO, and that is the whole use of it: run it, move one picture row, run it again, and the difference is what that row costs on your machine. Every run is kept under **BENCHMARK HISTORY** with the picture it was scored at written on its line as a bar a row, so the two sit side by side without anybody having had to plan for it; a row there opens that run's graph back up, and both the card and the list copy out as text — one run in full or the whole sheet with a legend under it. What a single run copies out with is the frame CUT INTO ITS PHASES: the field stepped at 120 Hz, the renderer's own half split into the water, the mirror, the wake and the submission, the fence the card is waited for with, and what the browser took that none of those did. Every one of them is meaned over all eighteen hundred frames rather than read off a single one, because a browser rounds its clock to a whole millisecond and a pass costing a third of one is otherwise never read as itself — and the machine's own line above says what that rounding actually is, beside the core count and the driver's name. Under it go the two figures no walk of the scene can take: the hulls on the water and the wash sources in it, whose PRODUCT is the shape of what the simulation costs.

**The campaign.** The front door's first tile, and the game with something to ride for. Four SHORES, nine LEVELS each — the warm mangrove coast first, the cold taiga behind it, the polar arctic behind that, the limestone karst behind that — every level a pinned seed on a pinned day (its hour, its season, its sky, its wind and the swell standing off the coast, the same every time it is ridden, so a time on it is a time on that water) with a name for what it is like rather than where it is: GLASS WATER, LOW HOPS, LAZY LAPS, SWELL HOPS, LONG SWELL, WET KICKERS, SQUALL LINE, BIG AIR, RUNNING DARK, on the cold shore COLD CALM, GRANITE KICKERS, IRON LAPS, SKERRY KICKERS, HEAD SEA, WINTER LIGHT, GREY SEA, HARD WINTER, STORM GRANITE, on the polar one MIDNIGHT SUN, SEA SMOKE, FLOE LAPS, BERG HOPS, NARROW LEAD, SNOW KICKERS, WHITE OUT, LAST LIGHT, BLACK WATER, and on the limestone one BLUE WATER, HEAT HAZE, ISLET LAPS, REEF KICKERS, SOUTHERLY, LIMESTONE KICKERS, BLACK WALL, BROKEN WATER, STORM REEF. Each shore climbs, and it alternates all the way up: a race, then a tricks run, five races around four tricks runs, so the race both opens and closes a shore — a race to open on, two minutes of ramps, a lapped circuit, three minutes of ramps, a harder race, four minutes of ramps, the race in the dark, six minutes of air, and last of all a race down the worst water that coast has in the final light of its season. Each climbs on what its own coast has: the warm shore's is the swell that came in from somebody else's weather, the cold one's a head sea among granite, the polar one's the ice — bergs on the line from the first rung, three laps out past the glacier's front, and, at the fifth, the one thing no other coast can put under a run: a sea frozen right over, with an icebreaker's channel cut down the course to ride and the sheet grounding a hull that leaves it — and the limestone one's the rock and the short steep sea the wind stands up off the land: islets and reefs on the line from flat blue water on, the tightest lap in the campaign, the southerly's grey chop, and a storm off the mountains to close on — and every level is ridden against the same eleven riders, who are on the water to be raced among and never leaned on: the hulls pass through each other, so a flip is never put in the water by somebody else's line. A level pays the podium the way a kart game does — three points for the win, two for second, one for third, nothing below — for the whole field, and the points are the locks: the next LEVEL opens once you finish on the podium (on a TRICKS level, once you earn a MEDAL — bronze, silver or gold are scores to beat, and bronze is the door), and the next SHORE opens once you have ridden all nine of this one and stand top of its table. Nothing is ever spent: ride a level again and the board keeps the better afternoon, which is why a cleared level is still worth going back for. The card asks the coast first, the same banner rows the level card opens on — with how far each shore's table has got written on it — and then shows that shore's nine boxes with what each has paid you and the layout of its water behind the words, and the table under them. RIDE stays in the corner on both steps and takes the box the campaign would pick next — the first you have not ridden, or the first you have not won — so a returning player never has to choose a coast to find where they stopped. Your board is kept on this machine (`sea-haven-campaign`).

**Four ways onto the water.** They are the front door's next four tiles. The first three ride the campaign's pinned shores, chosen on the level card; the fourth picks a seed. **RACE** puts eleven others on the grid with you — the whole roster, on the same stock hulls — holds everybody under three lights and lets the field go on one GO; the hulls can be leaned on (a shoulder off-centre swings a nose), your place reads top left beside the clock, and the last gate says where you finished. The tricks are off in a race: a lean held back in the air is trim, and the score and the air clock stay off the screen. **TRICKS** is the same shore with the course taken off it and a TRICK FIELD laid down it (R35): no gates, no finish, no rings — a ring is a checkpoint, and a run with no course to count has nothing to check — but a line of ramps down the racing line and back up it, three to four times as many lips as a race carries, each one a full run-up apart so you arrive at every lip at a pace worth leaving the water at. The same three lights hold the start before a clock runs DOWN from the two, four or six minutes the LENGTH row gives you and a score runs up; and a reset puts you at the foot of the nearest ramp's run-up. **TIME TRIAL** is the race with the field taken off — the course, the lights, the clock, nobody else. **FREE RIDE** is the water with nothing asked of you at all: the same three lights count you in — every way onto the water does — the gates still stand and the tricks still score, but no buzzer ends it and nobody else is out there — and it is the one card where the WIND and the WAVES are faders rather than ladders of words. Set forty metres a second, or twenty metres of swell rolling into a flat calm; the wind is always dead onshore, so neither row can be quietly cancelled by the other. It is also the one mode that rides a speed class other than stock, since nothing on it is being compared. Nothing a free ride does is kept, and the card says so where the other three show a best. The best this machine has seen on a shore in each of the other three modes is kept (`records.ts`) and shown on that shore's box on the level card; beat it and the plate at the end of a run says NEW BEST. **And on a TRICKS run or a TIME TRIAL, your best comes back out as a GHOST**: a see-through craft — the hull you set the figure on — riding that shore again exactly the way you rode it, with the gap to it beside the clock, in points on a tricks run and in seconds down a course. It is a picture and not a rival: nothing can be leaned on, and nothing it does counts for anything. It is your own run and nobody else's, kept on this machine, and a better run replaces it. Those two modes have one because they are the two ridden ALONE — a race and a campaign rung have eleven other hulls out there to be measured against instead.

**What a run is.** The course is a line of gates, in order. A **water gate** is two buoys; cross the line between them, the right way round, and it counts. An **air gate** is a ring hanging over the water with a floating **ramp** moored before it: hit the ramp with pace, lean back, and fly through the ring. **The benefit of the doubt is yours**: a checkpoint counts if any part of the craft or the rider went through the opening — a rail past the buoy, a helmet up through the hoop — and then a little more on top, so a crossing too close to call is one you are given. Cross the checkpoint's line outside its opening and it is missed immediately: five seconds go on the clock and the run moves on. If you are already past it, taking one of the next few checkpoints charges the ones skipped on the way. The last gate is the finish and must be crossed through its opening. **R** puts you back at the last gate you passed, facing the next one; **B** restarts the whole run on the same shore. **When the run ends** — at the finish line, or at the buzzer on a timed one — the craft coasts and a plate goes up saying so: what the run was worth, what it was measured against, and the three ways on — **RIDE AGAIN** on the same shore (which is what B presses), **WATCH REPLAY** where the run left a recording, and **MAIN MENU**. Escape still holds the run under the pause card, which offers the same three.

**Choosing a level.** Every level is generated from a seed, so a URL is a level — and naming one takes the pinned shore off, so a link rides the seed it asks for in every mode: `?seed=38` opens seed 38 (`?biome=mangrove` builds it on the warm coast), and `?craft=marlin` picks the craft (`skiff`, `marlin`, `otter`, `dart`); `?camera=heli` opens on a rung of the camera ladder; add `?start=1` to skip the cards and ride it. The seed is in the HUD corner, beside the build label — send both with a bug report and whoever reads it stands on the same water. The whole set of parameters is in [configuration.md](configuration.md).

**The craft.** Four ship, and what separates them is real physics off a data card rather than a badge: the **skiff** (a light runabout — quick, nimble, skittish in chop), the **marlin** (heavy performance — the fastest, and it needs room), the **otter** (stable touring — the heaviest, the softest over waves, slow to turn) and the **dart** (a stand-up — the lightest, the most agile, the least stable). The rider on the saddle is drawn from the engine's own readings: the lean you input moves a point mass in the physics, and the figure slides and leans where that mass went, tucks into the wind as the pump opens, hangs into a turn, and takes a landing in his knees and back.

### Controls

**Desktop (keyboard).**

| Key          | Action                                                                                                |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| W            | Throttle — analogue in spirit: it ramps up while held                                                 |
| ← → / A D    | Steer — ramped, so a tap is a nudge and a hold is full lock                                           |
| ↓            | Lean back — nose up; in the air, pitch the nose up                                                    |
| ↑ / Q        | Lean forward — nose down; in the air, pitch the nose down                                             |
| Shift (hold) | **Tuck** — get down behind the bars: less air resistance, less of everything you steer with your body |
| S / Space    | Brake and reverse — the bucket over the jet                                                           |
| R            | Back to the last gate passed (also the HUD button)                                                    |
| B            | Restart the run from the beginning                                                                    |
| Enter        | Take a screenshot — filed, and copied to the clipboard                                                |
| C            | Camera: the next rung of the ladder (also the HUD button); in a replay it walks the TV camera too     |
| H            | The HUD off the water, and back — the same switch as OPTIONS ▸ HUD                                    |
| Escape       | Pause: hold the run and put the pause card up — and the way out of a replay                           |

The left hand gets the driving set — W on the throttle, S on the brake, A and D steering — and the arrow cluster is the handlebar: ← → steer it, ↑ ↓ lean on it, the same way the thumb's bar on a phone does — pull toward you for nose up, push away for nose down.

**None of it is fixed.** OPTIONS ▸ CONTROLS ▸ KEY BINDINGS lists every action on the craft and every press around a run; press a row, then press the key you want on it, and that one key becomes the whole binding. Escape backs out of a row without changing anything — which is why it is the one key the page will not bind, since it is the way out of every surface in the game. A key may end up on two actions at once if you want it there; the row says ALSO ON when it is, and both things happen on the press. RESET KEYS puts the shipped layout back without touching anything else you have chosen. The page is only offered on a machine with keys — a phone is shown the rest of OPTIONS and not this.

**The tuck.** Hold SHIFT and the rider gets down behind the bars. It takes about a fifth off the hole the craft makes in the air, which on flat calm water is worth about a km/h at the top — a watercraft is stopped by the water, not by the air, so do not expect a new machine. Punching into a blow is where it lands: drag goes as the closing speed squared, so into 14 m/s of head wind the skiff takes the last stretch to 90 km/h in half the time tucked. It costs you the turn, because on a watercraft you steer with your body as much as with the bars: tucked, you cannot slide back down the seat, hang off the side, sweep the bars their full throw, or throw your weight about in the air. The stand-up pays most, since that is nearly all it steers with. Getting down and back up takes a moment — it is a body, not a switch — so a tuck grabbed on the way into a buoy is still costing you at the apex. It is a keyboard control and has no touch equivalent: both thumbs are already busy.

**The HUD, out of the way.** **H** takes the readouts off the water and brings them back — the speed, the clock, the gate count, the map, the news column, all of it. It is the same switch as OPTIONS ▸ HUD and the one on the pause card's own OPTIONS panel, so it is remembered between runs: a rider who cleared the screen to watch a wave, or to take a picture of one, finds it still clear next time. It works over the pause card as well as over a moving run, since a held frame is exactly where the water is worth looking at uncovered — and the new-build notice is not a readout, so it stays in its corner either way.

On a menu card the arrow keys (or WASD) walk the rows, Enter or Space presses one, and Escape goes back a page.

**Screenshots.** **Enter** takes a picture of the frame you are looking at — the sea, the craft and the sky the renderer drew, with the HUD over it as it stood when you pressed, and the game's mark in the corner. **On a phone you take it the way you take every other picture on that phone** — the hardware's own shutter; there is no button for it over the water, because the top strip is the one part of the screen that has to stay clear of the sea. In the **App Store / Play Store app** the game is told when you do that and files its own copy alongside, so a picture taken with the side buttons lands in the gallery below as well as in the phone's camera roll (iOS anywhere, Android 14 and up — it costs no permission either way, and in a mobile browser the phone's screenshot stays the phone's alone). The picture lands a frame or three later and the news column says so; the run never stops for it. **Every picture also goes straight onto the clipboard**, so it can be pasted into a chat or a document without going through the gallery — the receipt reads PICTURE SAVED · COPIED once the clipboard has it, and PICTURE SAVED alone in a browser with no image writer (Firefox). Every picture is kept in the browser on the machine it was taken on — the last forty, oldest falling off — and **GALLERY** on the front door is where they are looked at, flipped through, and **shared**, **copied** or **saved** to a file. Nothing is ever uploaded anywhere.

**Options.** OPTIONS on the front door is every knob the game actually has and not one it does not, in five groups, each headed by a word and a mark and walked in the order a rider meets them: **CONTROLS** (the key bindings, and the vibration on a phone that has a motor), **RIDING** (the camera), **HUD** (the readouts, and the frame-rate counter), **SOUND** (one fader for every sound the game makes, reading OFF at the bottom of its travel) and **PICTURE** (the resolution, the detail, how much coast is drawn, the sea itself, whether you can see into it, and the cap on how often all of it is asked for). Every row is the same shape — the name, the value between two arrows, and under the value either the pips saying where on its ladder it stands or, on a fader, the track it stands on — so one row learned is every row read. **No row explains itself**: point at one, or walk onto it with the keys, and the line at the foot of the card names it and says what it does. Everything here takes effect on the sea behind the card as you press it, which is why the page stands over water that is still running.

**Pausing.** Press Escape, or press the **minimap** — on a phone the map is the way in, since there is no Escape key to reach for. The run stops exactly where it stands: the wave holds, the spray hangs, the clock waits. Under the head is **how the run has gone so far** — up to four figures, the first of them whatever this run is being ridden for (where you stand in the field on a race, the points on a tricks run) and the clock beside it, then the run's own high-water marks: the longest flight it has flown, the furthest one carried, the highest the hull has been. Those three are the reason the strip is there at all: the readouts over the water can only show you a record for the moment after you land it, because a rider at speed cannot read a number that is not happening now. Under that the card carries three presses, and a fourth wherever there is one to make — each on a row of its own read by its **mark**: **RESUME** (also what Escape and a press on the backdrop do, because a card opened by a thumb aiming for the map has to cost one press to leave), **OPTIONS**, and **MAIN MENU**, which hands the same craft back to the bot and comes back to the front door over the shore you were just on — with OPTIONS standing between the other two so a thumb aiming for the water is never one row's travel from the press that ends the run. On a RACE, a TRICKS run or a TIME TRIAL a **WATCH REPLAY** row stands under OPTIONS, marked and ruled in the shallows' own colour so it is found without being looked for, and it says what it costs out at the end of its line: watching the run so far ENDS it, the same way the front door does. OPTIONS there is a panel of its own carrying the handful of settings a rider actually stops mid-run for — the **camera** you cannot see out of, the **HUD** and the **frame-rate counter** in the way of the water, and the **sound** too loud for the room — each in the same group under the same mark as on the front door, and each answering on the held frame behind the card. Esc

**Watching it back.** Every RACE, TRICKS run and TIME TRIAL is recorded while you ride it, and there are two ways to watch one: **WATCH REPLAY** on the plate that goes up at the finish, and the same row on the pause card mid-run — which ends the run, and says so on the row. A FREE ride keeps no recording, for the same reason it keeps no record: its wind and its sea are yours to set, so no two runs down that shore are the same run.

What is kept is not a video. It is **the controls you rode on**, step by step, and the shore you rode them on — so the replay is the game's own frames, the same physics doing the same thing, down to the crest you caught and the rival you shouldered off the line. That is why it costs a couple of hundred kilobytes rather than a couple of hundred megabytes, and why it can be watched from any camera instead of only the one you rode in. The whole field is out there with you, riding exactly the race it rode. Nothing is written to disk: a recording lives as long as the tab does, and leaving the replay drops it.

A replay opens on the **TV camera** — a rung you cannot reach while riding, because it is framed for somebody watching rather than somebody steering. It is a lens planted out on the water, beside a moment the recording already knows is coming: it **cuts to the corner before the jump**, the way a broadcast does, so what you see is a camera standing waiting and a craft arriving into it out of the distance, growing in a long lens that opens right up as it comes past. Between the moments it hands back to the chase boom — a broadcast does not leave a rider on a tripod for a whole race — and the hand-back is flown rather than cut, so the shot becomes the ride again.

**What it cuts to is what you did**: a flight that stayed up, a flip or a spin or a corkscrew turned in one, another hull met at speed, and it going wrong — over the bars, on its back, or taken by the tornado. The best few are chosen and spaced, so a race gets punctuation rather than a slideshow. Over each of them **the picture runs slow**, easing down into the moment and back out of it, with SLOW in the corner so it never reads as a machine dropping frames. **C** still walks the camera — the whole ordinary ladder is there behind the broadcast — and **Escape**, the bar's **EXIT** or a pad's back button leaves for the front door, as does the recording simply running out. The readouts stay up over it: they are reading the recording, and the recording is the run.ape or the backdrop steps back to the card rather than out to the water. The full OPTIONS page is not offered here: a picture row is judged against a sea that is moving, and the one thing this card does is stop it.

There is no handbrake and no gear: a jet ski has a throttle, a nozzle, and — on most of them — a bucket. Which means the one thing to learn early: **no throttle, almost no steering**. The nozzle turns the thrust; with no thrust there is nothing to turn. Off the throttle the hull keeps going where it was pointed, with only the keel's small say in the matter — letting go is measurably the worst thing you can do to a corner, and it is the real machine's real hazard rather than a quirk of ours. To turn, stay on the gas. To turn when you are carrying too much speed for the buoy, brake INTO it and get back on the gas — the gate scrubs the speed and keeps the nozzle flowing, which is the only hand that does both, and a braked corner comes round in about half the water a throttled one needs.

**The brake is the bucket** — and it is how you make a corner at speed. S or Space (or dragging the right thumb up past the shut mark) swings a clamshell down over the jet: the flow goes forward and under instead of back, and the gate itself becomes a plate hanging in the water, which is most of the braking once you are moving. It stops the craft hard — about half a g, twice what letting go of the throttle gives you — puts the bow down while it does, and held past the stop it backs you up at a walking pace — and because the nozzle sits upstream of the gate, the steering works BACKWARDS in reverse. **Hold it with the bars turned and it is the tightest line the craft has**: the buried bow puts the forefoot and the front half of the keel into water a planing hull keeps them clear of, and the craft swings through a right angle in about half the water — and less time — than the same corner taken flat out. That is what the button is for once you already know how to stop. **And it bites twice as fast as it used to, both ways.** The first half of the gate's swing — the half that spoils the thrust, hangs the plate in the water and puts the bow down, which is most of what you feel — is driven across in about a seventh of a second instead of a third; only the deep half that actually drives you backwards keeps the old rate. Letting GO is quicker still, because there the jet blows the gate open for you: the whole travel is driven, and the gate is clear in 0.22 s instead of 0.45. So the brake is a thing to FLICK as well as a thing to hold, which is what makes it the tool for PLACING the craft rather than only for slowing it: miss a buoy, and the way back to it is a jab of brake with the bars already over and then straight onto the gas with the bars still over. The throttle arrives back WITH the gate instead of behind it — the key hands it straight back on the frame you lift it, with no ramp in the way — so the speed you shed between letting go of the brake and going forward again drops from 8.7 km/h to 4.7 on the skiff. That is the whole point of the flick: you already have the speed, and the craft is only floating while you brake. Quick fingers are what it pays: pull the lever only half way and you get the old slow swing all the way down, because a gate you are feathering is a gate you meant to feather. For the corner ITSELF, hold it — a jab places the nose, a held gate is the tight line. You can see it working: the water either side of the hull erupts white and the craft sits in a pool of foam that reaches past the bow, and the rev bar's caption reads BRAKE — REVERSE once you are backing up — in the lever's own alarm colour. **The stand-up has no bucket at all** — no brake and no reverse — which is part of what makes it the hardest of the four to place on a tight line.

**Phone (touch).** The LOWER-LEFT of the screen is the handlebar: touch anywhere and move your thumb — sideways travel steers, vertical travel leans (down toward you is back, nose up). The LOWER-RIGHT is the LEVER, and the touch itself is WIDE OPEN — a finger landing anywhere in that half is already asking for everything the pump has, which is both what you want almost all the time and what a watercraft's own sprung finger lever does: you hold it on and ease it out. The throw runs UP the glass from where you touched, and it is one throw with two halves — about ninety pixels closes the throttle, and about sixty past that pulls the brake and reverse, drawn in the alarm colour so a thumb never has to ask which half it is in. Both are analogue, only one can be open at a time, held for as long as the finger is down, and let go the moment it lifts. Because the anchor is full throttle rather than nothing, a grip low on the glass costs you nothing: there is no travel to spend off the bottom of the phone. Both overlays are drawn under the thumb that owns them. There is no tuck on touch — both thumbs are already on the bar and the lever, and a third zone would have to be reached by letting go of one of them. Both stop at the lower three fifths of the screen, so the readouts along the top — and the RESET and CAMERA buttons among them — keep their own presses instead of losing them to the throttle. The one press that does stand inside a zone is the new-build mark in the bottom-right corner, which takes its own small patch of the lever's glass on the rare day there is a new build to offer. Works in portrait and landscape.

### How a run reads

The HUD is the game's own type over the water: **speed** big in km/h, the **rpm bar** under it (no gear to show), the **altitude tape** standing on top of those two — a vertical track with the still-water line drawn a quarter of the way up it and a marker that rides: it sinks into a trough, climbs a face, and runs away up the tape on a jump, with the figure in metres travelling beside it so how high and how high exactly are the one glance. It reads from the flat calm rather than from the water beneath you, which is the point of it: out past the rim of the level, where the storm deals the biggest sea the roster can still fly over, being carried up a ten-metre face is genuinely ten metres up — and it is the same instrument that says how far the tornado past the far edge of that ocean has taken you. Most of the track is spent on the heights a rider actually reaches and the rest is spread thin out to the column's own, so the travel is compressed where the figure beside it is not. The pale tick left standing on the track is the **run's high-water mark**, your best against where you are now. To its right is the **wind meter**: a thick, faceted arrow on a tilted compass plate, turning as the local wind or the craft's heading changes, with the metres per second under it. Straight up is the wind going with the craft. Then there is the **run clock** and **gate n / N** top left with the **last split** beside them, and under that line the hour of the day and **how far offshore you are** in metres — the level's own reading of the distance to the water's edge, which is also the fetch the wind has had to build the sea you are riding, so it says what the water is about to do as much as where you are. And, once a flight has lasted long enough to be one, an **air time** counter across the top centre of the frame — over the nose, where you are already looking to aim the landing — that runs until you touch down, with the **jump length** in metres beside it. The two are the two halves of one jump and the score pays them alike: how long it held you up, and how far it carried you. Half a second is the line for both: a hop off a crest is not air time, so neither figure is in the frame through the chop. They **fade** in rather than arriving, because a number that popped into the middle of the frame at the moment the hull left the water would be a flinch where you wanted a reading, and they **grow as the flight goes on**, so the shape of the thing over your nose already tells you whether this one is big before you have read a digit. Take the run's **longest flight** or its **longest jump** — they are kept apart, because a pop off a crest and a flat drive off a lip are two different bests — and that half sticks where it is for a couple of seconds after you land instead of going out with the water, stopped on the number you flew, pulsing, with **RECORD** under it and a chime over the splash. The metres stand down while the hull is UNDER the water: the tile is counting how long you have been down there, and there is no distance in that. The top-right corner is the **minimap**: a square of sea around you, drawn as the coast actually is — the water in three depths under a running swell, so you can see the bottom coming up before you are on it; the shore in the green of the wood it carries, with the bare stone of a headland above the tree line; a pale surf line between the two; and the rocks, the reefs, the racing line and the gates over it, the one you owe picked out, with a chevron on the frame pointing at it when it is off the window. Miss one and **MISSED CHECKPOINT** holds in the upper centre with a solid arrow aimed at that checkpoint and the metres back beneath it, while a red circle beats around the checkpoint on the map **and its own buoys out on the water go over to that same red and beat with it** — the one thing in the frame that says WHICH checkpoint the warning means, since the lantern has already handed over to the gate ahead. **And the RESET press under the map lights up with them** — the disc goes to that same red and a ring beats out of it, because it is the press that answers the moment and the moment you are least likely to be remembering the key for. All four go out on the same step: the one you ride back inside the checkpoint's opening. Or take the RESET, which hands the checkpoint back — the run rewinds to the last one you actually took, the five seconds come back off the clock, and the stretch is yours to ride again; what it costs you is riding it a second time from a standing start. **The window breathes with the speedo** — it closes right in at an idle, where the map is a plan of the water under the hull, and opens to most of half a kilometre on the plane, where it is the sea about to arrive — so what it holds is a roughly constant amount of warning rather than a constant piece of sea, and the coast pulling back as you wind up is something the eye reads as speed. The rule at its foot says what the picture is worth. The frame is the progress gauge, the figure opposite the rule is how far the next gate is, and pressing it holds the run. Hung under the map are the two presses a rider makes with the hull still moving: **RESET** back to the last checkpoint you took, and **CAMERA** to the next view. They are discs rather than plates — the one shape in that corner that can only be a button, since everything else up there is read and never touched. The build label sits in the corner. Under all of it, when OPTIONS ▸ FPS asks for it, the frame rate — and under that, for anyone who has found the developer page, what the frame cost: the water's slice of the processor, the draw calls and the triangles. **The whole of it dims when the craft's lamp comes on.** The chrome was mixed to hold over a bright sea at noon, and over a coast lit by a headlamp and a moon the same white is glare in front of the dark you are trying to see into — so as the sun goes down the ink comes off pure white, the navy behind every figure goes to nearly black, and the chart's shore goes to slate. It rides the lamp rather than the clock, so it comes on with the beam and comes on the way the beam does, over the couple of minutes the sun takes to cross the horizon. What does not dim is what you would want woken by: the redline, the brake, a missed gate, your own mark on the chart, and the buoy orange of the gate you owe, which has to go on agreeing with the buoys out on the water.

**The water is the game.** The sea is a sum of real waves, built from the level's wind by a fetch-limited spectrum: even in the lee of the shore it runs half a metre, and the further out you ride the more the wind has had room to work on it, so the swell grows with distance from the coast — at a hull's pace a crest every second or so: the climb, the launch, the drop into the next face. It shoals as it comes in — steepening over the shallows, and breaking where it gets too tall for the depth under it. The hull floats on that surface by displacement, twelve probes reading the water under them; it lifts onto the plane as speed rises and the drag drops away through the hump; and it feels the orbital motion of the wave itself, so a crest gives it a shove and a trough takes one back. A hard landing decelerates hard; a nose-down landing DIVES — the bow buries, the drag spikes and the nose is dragged further down — which is what the lean is for.

**The air.** A ramp is a plane the hull rides up; leave it at speed and the craft is ballistic, with the wind leaning on it and the lean input pitching it. Level the hull before the water arrives and it lands clean; land nose-down and it dives.

**The backflip**, and how it is actually ridden: hold the lean back going up the face of a wave or up a ramp's deck, and then **TAP** it off the lip — every tap is another haul on the bars, and the hull comes round faster for each one. A hold alone is one haul and will only flip the lightest hull; the taps are what bring a **backflip** round off an ordinary ramp, in a tap or two on the stand-up and in six or seven on the tourer, because how much a haul is worth is the rider's own authority against the hull's pitch inertia. Stop tapping and it stops turning faster, which is how you aim the landing — and nothing is banked until the hull is back on the water under you.

**The corkscrew.** Throw the bars fully OVER while you are hauling them back and both axes come round in the same flight: the flip, the barrel roll, and a third element for having done them together, which is worth more than the two taken off two waves in a row. It is the trick to go looking for once you have both of the others — and it has to be landed, because a combo with a corkscrew in it is a combo that goes in the water with you.

**Riding the wave.** The one thing worth points that never leaves the water: run ALONG a wave with a metre or more in it and hold the top of it, and the score ticks while you stay up there. Take it across the sea instead and you are over the crest and down the back in half a second; run with it and you keep it for a couple; turn along it and you can hold it for three to five. Past a second it is an element in its own right, and it is the move that carries a combo from one wave to the next.

**The laydown.** Lay the hull right over on its side — past forty-five degrees, which a carve on flat water will not reach, so it wants a wave face or the hull's own roll under it — and bring it back up level. It is the cheapest of the elements and the only one you can turn anywhere, and it is a lean away from a capsize the whole time.

**Keeping the combo alive.** A combo stays open while you are DOING something — in the air, holding the top of a wave, or driven under by a following wave with the craft still answering — and for a second on the water after any of them. So a flip off this wave and a laydown off the next are one combo at one multiplier rather than two small ones, and stringing them is worth far more than landing them apart. Going over the bars ends it and pays nothing.

### Installing on your phone

The game is a PWA. iOS Safari: Share → **Add to Home Screen**. Android Chrome: menu → **Install app** (or accept the install prompt). The installed game launches fullscreen, works offline once loaded, and shows an in-app prompt when a new build ships.

## Developing

```sh
git clone https://github.com/niclaslindstedt/game3
cd game3
npm install
npm run dev
```

Then read [architecture.md](architecture.md) for the layout and [CONTRIBUTING.md](../CONTRIBUTING.md) for the workflow. The tools you'll live in while tuning: `make sim` (does the change help or hurt, measured), `make waves` (what the sea is), `make ride` (what the hull does on it), `make level` (what the generator builds), `make screenshots` (what it looks like).
