Riding at a ramp from the wrong side no longer launches the craft tens of metres into the air, tumbling. A ramp was modelled as a one-sided plane over its whole plan footprint, so a hull that met it through the end face under its raised lip was pushed along the deck's upward normal by the full height of the lip — thirty metres of air on a short ramp, over a hundred on a long one, at every speed. The ramp is now a wedge with walls under its deck: ridden at from a flank or from downrange, it stops the hull like the solid it is, and only the hinge end — where the deck meets the water — carries a hull up.
